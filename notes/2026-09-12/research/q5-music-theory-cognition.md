## Findings

### 1. Neo-Riemannian transformations, the Tonnetz, and the PLR group

**Transformational theory (Lewin 1987).** The formal ancestor of neo-Riemannian theory is Lewin's *Generalized Musical Intervals and Transformations* (GMIT), which defines a Generalized Interval System (GIS): a set S of musical objects, a group IVLS of "intervals", and a function int: S × S → IVLS satisfying (a) int(s,t)·int(t,u) = int(s,u) and (b) for every s and every interval i there is a unique t with int(s,t) = i. Lewin then shifts from "intervals between objects" to "transformations acting on objects" — group actions on a set — which is exactly the formal object an engineering notation would need if it wants to say "state A moves to state B via operation X". [Lewin1987 — UNVERIFIED details; the book's existence and content are standard.]

**Cohn's papers.** Cohn's 1996 *Music Analysis* paper ("Maximally smooth cycles, hexatonic systems, and the analysis of late-Romantic triadic progressions") introduces the idea of parsimonious (maximally smooth) voice-leading cycles among consonant triads; the 1997 *Journal of Music Theory* paper ("Neo-Riemannian operations, parsimonious trichords, and their Tonnetz representations") formalises the P, L, R operations on set-class 3-11 (major/minor triads) and shows why the consonant triad is uniquely suited to single-semitone voice-leading among trichords; the 1998 JMT special-issue introduction ("Introduction to neo-Riemannian theory: a survey and a historical perspective") is the standard survey; *Audacious Euphony* (2012, OUP) is the book-length synthesis, reviewed by Yust in MTO 18.3 (that review VERIFIED as existing). [Cohn1996, Cohn1997, Cohn1998 — UNVERIFIED bibliographic detail; Cohn2012 — VERIFIED existence via MTO review.]

**The P/L/R operations.** Acting on the 24 consonant (major and minor) triads:
- P (Parallel): C major ↔ C minor (moves the third by a semitone).
- L (Leading-tone exchange): C major ↔ E minor (root moves down a semitone to the leading tone).
- R (Relative): C major ↔ A minor (fifth moves up a whole tone).
Each is an involution (applying it twice returns the original triad), each preserves two common tones, and each moves the remaining voice by a semitone (P, L) or whole tone (R). These properties are what "parsimonious voice leading" means formally.

**Group structure.** Crans, Fiore & Satyendra (2009, *American Mathematical Monthly* 116(6)) — VERIFIED via the Taylor & Francis record and the arXiv abstract — prove that the group generated by P, L and R (the "PLR group") is the dihedral group of order 24, and that it acts simply transitively on the set of 24 consonant triads. The transposition/inversion (T/I) group is also dihedral of order 24, acting on the same set. The arXiv abstract (fetched) states that since the 1970s theorists have modeled transposition and inversion as an action of the dihedral group of order 24, that there is a second such action (the PLR action), and that "these two actions are dual" — in the body the duality is made precise as each group being the centralizer of the other in the symmetric group on the 24 triads (this centralizer statement is from the paper's body and is UNVERIFIED this session as a verbatim claim). The PLR group is generated by L and R alone; P is expressible as a word in L and R. [CransFioreSatyendra2009 — VERIFIED.]

**The Tonnetz.** The neo-Riemannian Tonnetz is a (usually toroidal, under 12-tone equal temperament) lattice of pitch classes with axes of perfect fifths, major thirds and minor thirds; consonant triads are its triangles, and P, L, R are the three "edge-flips" of a triangle to its neighbour across a shared edge. Douthett & Steinbach (1998, JMT) generalise this as parsimonious graphs (the "Chicken-wire torus", "Cube Dance", etc.). Tymoczko (2011, *A Geometry of Music*) embeds this in the orbifold geometry of voice-leading spaces, which is the appropriate continuous generalisation if the treatise wants metric distances between chords rather than discrete operations. [DouthettSteinbach1998, Tymoczko2011 — UNVERIFIED.]

**Hexatonic and octatonic cycles.** Alternating P and L generates a 6-cycle of triads whose union of pitch classes is a hexatonic collection (e.g. C major – C minor – A♭ major – A♭ minor – E major – E minor – back to C major); there are four such "hexatonic systems" (Cohn 1996). Alternating P and R generates an 8-cycle whose pitch-class union is an octatonic collection (three such systems). Alternating L and R generates a 24-cycle through all consonant triads (the "LR-cycle"). These cycles are the cosets/orbits of the subgroups ⟨P,L⟩, ⟨P,R⟩ and ⟨L,R⟩ inside the dihedral group; they are therefore a clean, citable way to define "closed loops in state space" with different periods (6, 8, 24). [Cohn1996, Cohn1997, CransFioreSatyendra2009.]

**Relevance for an engineering notation.** What neo-Riemannian theory gives is a rigorous discrete-group model of *moves between states* with (a) a fixed finite state set, (b) involutive generators, (c) a distance metric (word length in the Cayley graph; or voice-leading distance), and (d) named closed cycles. It does *not* give any empirical claim about what those moves feel like; Cohn's writing about the "uncanny" affect of hexatonic poles (2004, JAMS "Uncanny resemblances") is hermeneutic, not experimental. [Cohn2004 — UNVERIFIED.]

### 2. Tonal tension, key-finding, and dissonance/roughness

**Lerdahl's Tonal Pitch Space (2001) and Lerdahl & Krumhansl (2007).** *Tonal Pitch Space* (OUP 2001) defines a multi-level pitch-space distance (chromatic → diatonic → triadic → fifths/root levels) between chords in keys, and from it derives (i) sequential tension, (ii) hierarchical tension (via GTTM prolongational reduction), (iii) surface dissonance, and (iv) melodic/harmonic attraction. Lerdahl & Krumhansl (2007), *Music Perception* 24(4), beginning p. 329 — VERIFIED as to venue, volume, issue and first page via the UC Press record — test these predictions against continuous tension ratings from listeners for excerpts spanning Bach to Messiaen; they report that the hierarchical model combined with attraction and dissonance terms fits listener data well, and better than the purely sequential model. The exact correlation coefficients were not seen this session and are UNVERIFIED. Bigand, Parncutt & Lerdahl (1996, *Perception & Psychophysics*) is the earlier chord-sequence tension experiment. [Lerdahl2001, BigandParncuttLerdahl1996 — UNVERIFIED; LerdahlKrumhansl2007 — VERIFIED record.]

**Farbood (2012).** "A parametric, temporal model of musical tension", *Music Perception* 29(4), beginning p. 387 — VERIFIED as to venue/volume/issue/first page via UC Press URL and NYU Scholars record. Farbood's model treats tension as a time-varying function of several parametric features (harmonic tension, melodic contour/pitch height, loudness, onset frequency/tempo, and others), each weighted and combined with an attentional/temporal windowing scheme; weights are fitted from listener continuous-response data. This is the most directly reusable "engineering" tension model because it is explicitly multi-feature and time-windowed. Later work (TenseMusic, 2024, PMC10798497 — VERIFIED existence) automates the prediction. [Farbood2012 — VERIFIED record.]

**Krumhansl key profiles and the Krumhansl–Schmuckler key-finding algorithm.** The key profiles come from Krumhansl & Kessler (1982, *Psychological Review*), using the probe-tone method; the key-finding algorithm (correlate a pitch-class duration vector with the 24 key profiles, pick the max) is presented in Krumhansl's *Cognitive Foundations of Musical Pitch* (1990, OUP), attributed to Krumhansl and Schmuckler. Temperley (1999, *Music Perception*) critiques and modifies it (Bayesian re-interpretation; modified profiles). The important engineering fact is that this is a linear correlation classifier over a 12-vector, easy to reproduce and cite. [KrumhanslKessler1982, Krumhansl1990, Temperley1999 — UNVERIFIED.]

**Roughness / sensory dissonance.** Plomp & Levelt (1965, JASA) proposed that sensory dissonance of pure-tone pairs is a function of frequency separation relative to the critical bandwidth, with maximum roughness near a quarter of a critical band and consonance for separations beyond it; complex-tone dissonance is the sum over partial pairs. Sethares (*Tuning, Timbre, Spectrum, Scale*, Springer, 2nd ed. 2005; and his 1993 JASA paper) parameterises this curve and shows that the consonance of intervals depends on the spectrum of the timbre — a "dissonance curve" is a property of a (timbre, interval) pair, not of the interval alone. This matters for the treatise: "dissonance" is not a scale-degree property; it is spectrum-dependent. [PlompLevelt1965, Sethares1993, Sethares2005 — UNVERIFIED bibliographic detail.]

**Caveat on consonance universality.** McDermott, Schultz, Undurraga & Godoy (2016, *Nature*) reported that the Tsimane' of Bolivia show no preference for consonant over dissonant chords, and McDermott, Lehr & Oxenham (2010, *Current Biology*) showed individual differences in consonance preference track harmonicity rather than roughness. A treatise must therefore separate *roughness* (a psychoacoustic quantity with a plausible physiological basis) from *consonance preference/valence* (culturally shaped). [McDermott2010, McDermott2016 — UNVERIFIED.]

**Huron (2006), *Sweet Anticipation* — ITPRA.** Huron's ITPRA theory decomposes the affective response to an expected event into five components: Imagination, Tension (pre-outcome arousal), Prediction (reward for correct prediction), Reaction (fast, defensive appraisal) and Appraisal (slower, conscious evaluation). It is a theory of expectation, grounded in statistical learning, and the most cited framework for saying "tension is anticipatory arousal before an outcome". It provides vocabulary, not a fitted model. [Huron2006 — UNVERIFIED details.]

### 3. Metre and rhythm

**GTTM (Lerdahl & Jackendoff 1983).** *A Generative Theory of Tonal Music* gives four hierarchical components: grouping structure, metrical structure (well-formedness and preference rules over a grid of beats at multiple levels — the dot notation), time-span reduction and prolongational reduction. Metrical well-formedness rules (e.g. beats at each level equally spaced; every beat at a level is a beat at all smaller levels) are exactly the kind of formal constraints a notation could adopt to define "regular" vs "irregular" operational rhythms. [LerdahlJackendoff1983 — UNVERIFIED.]

**London (2004/2012), *Hearing in Time*.** London treats metre as a form of entrainment, with well-formedness constraints for metric cycles and perceptual limits: a lower bound on the perceivable inter-onset interval (~100 ms, the "subdivision" floor), a preferred beat range centred around 500–700 ms, and an upper bound for metrical hearing of ~1.5–2 s between beats (the exact figures cited by London are from Fraisse and others; treat as UNVERIFIED this session). He also introduces "many meters hypothesis" and non-isochronous meters. [London2004 — UNVERIFIED.]

**Dynamic Attending Theory (Large & Jones 1999; Jones & Boltz 1989).** Large & Jones (1999, *Psychological Review*) model attention as a set of self-sustaining oscillators that entrain to rhythmic input, with an attentional pulse whose width narrows as the rhythm becomes predictable; attention is therefore heightened at expected time points. Jones & Boltz (1989) is the earlier "future-oriented attending" statement. This is the standard justification for saying "regular pulse → focused, anticipatory attention; irregular pulse → diffuse attention". [LargeJones1999, JonesBoltz1989 — UNVERIFIED.]

**Tempo and arousal — evidence.** Tempo is the single most consistent acoustic correlate of perceived arousal/activity in both music and speech in Juslin & Laukka's (2003) meta-analysis (VERIFIED record): fast tempo/speech rate and high intensity are associated with high-arousal emotions (anger, happiness, fear), slow tempo/low intensity with sadness and tenderness. Husain, Thompson & Schellenberg (2002, *Music Perception*) manipulated tempo and mode of a Mozart sonata and found tempo affected arousal and mode affected mood, with arousal mediating a spatial-task effect. Physiological entrainment claims (heart-rate following tempo) are weaker and mixed; do not overclaim. [JuslinLaukka2003 — VERIFIED; HusainThompsonSchellenberg2002 — UNVERIFIED.]

### 4. Music and emotion

**Hevner (1936, 1937).** Hevner's adjective-circle studies (*American Journal of Psychology*) had listeners assign adjectives to pieces altered in mode, tempo, rhythm, harmony, melody direction; she found mode (major = happy/graceful; minor = sad/dreamy) and tempo (fast = exciting/happy; slow = dignified/sad) to be the strongest determinants, with harmony (simple vs complex/dissonant) next. Historically foundational; small samples by modern standards. [Hevner1936, Hevner1937 — UNVERIFIED.]

**Gabrielsson & Lindström (2001; rev. 2010).** Chapter "The role of structure in the musical expression of emotions" (in Juslin & Sloboda, eds., *Music and Emotion* 2001 and *Handbook of Music and Emotion* 2010, OUP) is the standard review table of structural factor → emotion associations across ~100 studies: tempo (fast → activity/happiness/anger; slow → sadness/tenderness/solemnity), mode (major → happiness; minor → sadness), loudness (loud → power/anger; soft → tenderness/sadness), pitch height, articulation (staccato → activity; legato → tenderness/sadness), timbre (many harmonics → anger/potency; few → tenderness), harmony (consonant → pleasant; dissonant → tension/anger), rhythm regularity, and so on. It is careful to note interactions (e.g. tempo dominates mode). [GabrielssonLindstrom2010 — UNVERIFIED.]

**Juslin & Laukka (2003).** *Psychological Bulletin* 129(5), 770–814, DOI 10.1037/0033-2909.129.5.770 — VERIFIED (APA DOI seen in a Google Scholar lookup record, PMID 12956543). Meta-analysis of 104 vocal-expression and 41 music-performance studies: decoding accuracy for the five basic emotions (anger, fear, happiness, sadness, tenderness) is well above chance in both channels, and the acoustic cues used overlap: speech rate/tempo, intensity/loudness, high-frequency energy/timbre brightness, F0/pitch level and variability, attack/articulation. This is the strongest single citation for a "shared code" between vocal affect and musical performance cues. The specific accuracy percentages were not seen this session (UNVERIFIED).

**Juslin & Västfjäll (2008).** *Behavioral and Brain Sciences* 31(5) — VERIFIED as existing at Cambridge Core; the target-article DOI 10.1017/S0140525X08005293 is inferred from the Cambridge file name and should be double-checked. The paper proposes that musical emotions are induced via distinct mechanisms — brain-stem reflex, evaluative conditioning, emotional contagion, visual imagery, episodic memory, and musical expectancy (later BRECVEMA, adding rhythmic entrainment and aesthetic judgment; Juslin 2013, *Physics of Life Reviews* — UNVERIFIED). Its importance for the treatise: it distinguishes *perceived* emotion (recognising what music expresses) from *induced* emotion (feeling it), and warns that different mechanisms have different time courses and different reliabilities. A notation that claims to *communicate* state relies on perceived emotion, which is far better established than induced emotion.

**Eerola & Vuoskoski (2013).** *Music Perception* 30(3), beginning p. 307 — VERIFIED record. The review covered 251 studies (VERIFIED from the fetched page); about 70% used either discrete or dimensional (valence–arousal) emotion models (VERIFIED). They argue for the dimensional model's robustness and note stimulus choices are heterogeneous. Their companion work (Eerola & Vuoskoski 2011, *Psychology of Music*, on discrete vs dimensional models with film music) is the usual citation for the finding that valence and arousal ratings are reliable and that "tension" behaves partly as a third dimension. [EerolaVuoskoski2011 — UNVERIFIED.]

**Cross-cultural robustness.** Fritz et al. (2009, *Current Biology*) found that Mafa listeners with no Western-music exposure recognised happy/sad/scared in Western music above chance, using tempo and mode cues; Balkwill & Thompson (1999, *Music Perception*) found Western listeners judged emotion in Hindustani ragas using tempo, rhythmic complexity and melodic complexity. These support tempo (arousal) as more culturally robust than mode (valence). [Fritz2009, BalkwillThompson1999 — UNVERIFIED.]

### 5. Prior use of musical notation as a formal representation of non-musical state

**Xenakis, *Formalized Music* (1971; rev. 1992, Pendragon).** Xenakis's direction is the reverse of the treatise's: he imports formal systems (stochastic processes, Markov chains, game theory, group theory, sieve theory, Boolean algebra) into composition, so that a score becomes the trace of a formal process (e.g. *Pithoprakta*'s glissandi as gas-molecule velocities; *Achorripsis*'s Poisson-distributed events). The relevance is precedent for treating a score as a rigorous encoding of a mathematical state process, and his "sieve theory" is itself a group-theoretic notation for scales/rhythms. Also relevant: his UPIC system (graphic → sound). [Xenakis1992 — UNVERIFIED.]

**Data sonification via musical structure.** The sonification field (Kramer et al. 1999 NSF report; Hermann, Hunt & Neuhoff, eds., *The Sonification Handbook*, 2011, Logos, open access) distinguishes audification, parameter-mapping sonification and model-based sonification. "Musical" sonification — mapping data to pitch, rhythm, harmony within a tonal framework and, sometimes, rendering it as notation — has a documented lineage:
- Accentus LLC's US Patent 7,138,575, "System and method for musical sonification of data" (Childs, Perkins & Brooks; filed 2003, granted 2006) — VERIFIED via the Justia record fetched this session. It sonifies real-time financial market data, maps data to pitch, volume, time signature, tempo, duration, envelope, timbre, spatialisation and effects, and its figures show musical-notation renderings of discrete and continuous sonifications. Sibling patents 7,135,635 and 7,629,528 exist (VERIFIED as listed). This is a direct, citable precedent for "notation as a representation of non-musical time-series state" — in the financial-market domain.
- The R package `musicXML` on CRAN ("Data Sonification using 'musicXML'") — VERIFIED existence — renders data as MusicXML scores, i.e. notation as the sonification target.
- Peep, "the network auralizer" (Gilfix & Couch, USENIX LISA 2000) — sonified network state with ambient natural sounds rather than notation; it is the standard citation for system-monitoring sonification, and its non-musical choice is itself informative. [GilfixCouch2000 — UNVERIFIED.]
- Medical sonification: the pulse oximeter's variable-pitch tone (a de facto ISO/IEC standard, see q3 file) is the most successful clinical sonification; ECG-to-melody and EEG sonification papers exist (e.g. Hermann et al. on EEG; Ballora et al. on heart-rate variability, 2004, *Leonardo*) but these mostly map to sound, not to notation. An editorial in *Frontiers*, "Sonification, aesthetic representation of physical quantities" (2023, PMC10064135 — VERIFIED existence), surveys the field. [Ballora2004 — UNVERIFIED.]

**Assessment.** There is precedent for (a) formal processes → score (Xenakis), (b) time-series data → musical parameters and occasionally → notation (Accentus, musicXML), and (c) system state → sound (Peep). I found no prior work that uses *staff notation as a psychometric or operational-state representation* in the sense the treatise proposes (psychological state → harmony/metre/orchestration symbols intended to be read, not necessarily heard). That gap should be stated explicitly as the treatise's claimed novelty, with the caveat that the search here was limited (8 queries).

### 6. Orchestration/timbre → affect mappings with evidence

- **Timbre and affect dimensions.** Eerola, Ferrer & Alluri (2012, *Music Perception* 30(1)) rated isolated instrument tones for valence/arousal/tension and found affect ratings correlated with acoustic features: spectral centroid/brightness and attack time predicted arousal; ratio of high to low spectral energy and spectral flux predicted tension. Hailstone et al. (2009, *Quarterly Journal of Experimental Psychology*) showed the same melodies played on different instruments (piano, violin, trumpet, synthetic) shifted recognised emotion — timbre alone alters perceived emotion. [EerolaFerrerAlluri2012, Hailstone2009 — UNVERIFIED.]
- **Performance-cue evidence.** Juslin & Laukka (2003, VERIFIED) list timbre (high-frequency energy, "sharpness") as a cue: more high-frequency energy for anger/happiness, less for sadness/tenderness.
- **Structure reviews.** Gabrielsson & Lindström (2010) summarise: many-harmonics/bright timbres → potency/anger/activity; few harmonics/soft timbres → tenderness/sadness/pleasantness. [UNVERIFIED.]
- **Orchestration treatises** (Berlioz/Strauss, Rimsky-Korsakov *Principles of Orchestration*, Adler *The Study of Orchestration*) contain the craft mappings (e.g. brass = power/ceremony, low strings/tremolo = menace, muted strings = intimacy) but are not empirical; McAdams and collaborators' ACTOR project (2018–) is the ongoing empirical program on orchestration perception (blend, segregation, contrast) — cite as descriptive craft knowledge, not evidence. [Adler, RimskyKorsakov, McAdams-ACTOR — UNVERIFIED.]
- **Timbre space.** Grey (1977, JASA) and McAdams et al. (1995, *Psychological Research*) give the multidimensional-scaling timbre space (attack time, spectral centroid, spectral flux) that the affect studies above build on. [Grey1977, McAdams1995 — UNVERIFIED.]

## What an engineering treatise can legitimately say

- **Tempo → arousal: well supported.** Across the Juslin & Laukka (2003) meta-analysis, Hevner, Gabrielsson & Lindström's review, Husain et al. (2002), and cross-cultural work (Fritz et al. 2009), tempo/event rate is the most robust and most culturally stable cue to perceived arousal/activity. The treatise can map an operational "activity/urgency" axis to tempo with confidence, citing JuslinLaukka2003 and Eerola & Vuoskoski 2013 for the dimensional model.
- **Mode → valence: supported for Western-enculturated listeners, weaker cross-culturally, and dominated by tempo when the two conflict.** Cite Hevner 1936, Gabrielsson & Lindström, Husain et al. 2002; note that Fritz et al. 2009 and Balkwill & Thompson 1999 show mode is less universal than tempo. Say "major/minor is a *conventional* valence marker" rather than a universal one.
- **Dissonance/roughness → tension: supported as a *component* of tension, not as tension itself.** Plomp–Levelt/Sethares roughness is a measurable psychoacoustic quantity; Lerdahl & Krumhansl (2007) and Farbood (2012) both include surface dissonance as one predictor of continuous tension ratings alongside harmonic distance, attraction, loudness, onset rate and pitch height. Do not claim that dissonance maps to negative valence universally (McDermott et al. 2016).
- **Harmonic distance → tension/instability: supported within the tonal idiom.** Tonal Pitch Space distances and the Krumhansl key profiles are empirically fitted and predict tension ratings and key perception; the treatise may use a pitch-space distance as a "distance from home state" metric, with the caveat that fits are for Western tonal listeners.
- **Neo-Riemannian operations → a formal state-transition algebra: legitimate as mathematics, not as psychology.** The PLR group is provably D24 (Crans, Fiore & Satyendra 2009, VERIFIED); hexatonic/octatonic cycles are its subgroup orbits. The treatise can use this as a rigorous discrete model of state transitions and cycles, but should not claim listeners perceive P, L, R transformations as distinct affects without further evidence.
- **Regular metre → focused anticipatory attention; metric irregularity → uncertainty: supported at the level of theory and psychophysics** (Large & Jones 1999 dynamic attending; London's entrainment limits; GTTM metrical rules), but the affective interpretation ("irregular metre = anxiety") is an inference, not a measured finding — label it as such.
- **Timbre → arousal/tension: moderately supported** (Eerola, Ferrer & Alluri 2012; Hailstone et al. 2009; Juslin & Laukka 2003 timbre cues). Brightness/spectral centroid tracks arousal; timbre alone shifts perceived emotion. Instrument-family → affect mappings from orchestration treatises are craft heuristics with no controlled evidence and should be labelled as such.
- **Perceived vs induced emotion must be kept separate** (Juslin & Västfjäll 2008). A notation intended to *communicate* state only needs perceived-emotion evidence, which is much stronger; claims that reading or hearing the notation will *induce* the state in the operator are not supported.

## Gaps

- No verified study was found that uses staff notation itself (rather than audio) as a communicative representation of non-musical state and tests reader comprehension; the treatise's novelty claim rests on this gap and it should be framed cautiously given the limited search here.
- Exact effect sizes, correlation coefficients and decoding-accuracy percentages for Lerdahl & Krumhansl 2007, Farbood 2012 and Juslin & Laukka 2003 were not seen this session and are deliberately omitted; they must be pulled from the papers before being printed.
- Publisher pages for UC Press and Taylor & Francis returned 403 to the fetch tool; DOIs for the UC Press *Music Perception* papers are given in the standard 10.1525/mp.YEAR.VOL.ISS.PAGE pattern and are marked as pattern-inferred.
- Empirical evidence for affective differences between specific neo-Riemannian transformations (P vs L vs R) or between hexatonic vs octatonic cycles is essentially absent; Cohn's affective readings are hermeneutic.
- Physiological entrainment to tempo (heart rate, respiration) has a mixed literature and was not researched here.
- The consonance-universality debate (McDermott et al. 2016 and subsequent replications/critiques, e.g. Bowling et al.) was not verified this session.
