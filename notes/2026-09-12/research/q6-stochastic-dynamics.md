### 1. Kramers escape-rate theory (the "barrier escape" the corpus already uses)

**Primary source.** Kramers, H. A. (1940). "Brownian motion in a field of force and the diffusion model of chemical reactions." *Physica* 7(4), 284–304. DOI 10.1016/S0031-8914(40)90098-2 — **VERIFIED** (ScienceDirect PII S0031891440900982, ADS 1940Phy.....7..284K). [Kramers1940]

**Canonical review.** Hänggi, P., Talkner, P., & Borkovec, M. (1990). "Reaction-rate theory: fifty years after Kramers." *Reviews of Modern Physics* 62(2), 251–341. DOI 10.1103/RevModPhys.62.251 — **VERIFIED** (APS page; full PDF fetched from a Weizmann course mirror, header reads "Reviews of Modern Physics, Vol. 62, No. 2, April 1990"). [HTB1990]

**The physical picture (HTB1990, §I).** A Brownian particle of mass M sits in a metastable well of a potential U(x), coupled to a heat bath at temperature T with friction (damping) coefficient γ. ω0 is the angular frequency of small oscillations in the well (ω0² = U''(x_min)/M), ωb the "barrier frequency" at the saddle (ωb² = |U''(x_b)|/M), and Eb the barrier height. The escape rate k is the inverse mean first-passage time over the barrier. Validity condition: **Eb ≫ kT** (HTB1990 write it as βEb ≫ 1, their Eq. 2.2b, with β = 1/kT) — i.e. the theory is a *rare-event* theory; it does not apply when the barrier is comparable to the noise.

**Formulas.** As transcribed from the fetched HTB1990 PDF (equation numbering as reported by the extraction; the intermediate-friction form was returned in a slightly garbled shape by the fetch tool, so it is given below in the textbook form and flagged):

- Overdamped / strong-friction (Smoluchowski) limit — HTB1990 Eq. (1.6), transcribed:
  **k = (ω0 ωb / 2πγ) · exp(−Eb / kT)**
  This is exactly the formula quoted in the question. It is the one relevant to "operator collapse" analogies because human-state variables (fatigue, arousal, workload) are always modelled as heavily damped.

- Moderate-to-strong friction (Kramers' full result) — HTB1990 Eq. (1.5); the standard form (Gardiner, *Handbook of Stochastic Methods*, ch. 5; Risken, *The Fokker-Planck Equation*, ch. 5) is
  k = { [ (γ²/4 + ωb²)^{1/2} − γ/2 ] / ωb } · (ω0 / 2π) · exp(−Eb / kT)
  which reduces to (1.6) for γ ≫ ωb and to the transition-state-theory (TST) rate k_TST = (ω0/2π)·exp(−Eb/kT) for γ → 0 from above. **Textbook-standard; the fetched extraction of (1.5) was not clean enough to quote verbatim.**

- Weak-damping (energy-diffusion) limit — HTB1990 Eq. (1.4): k ∝ γ β I_b · k_TST for γβI_b ≪ 1, where I_b is the action at the barrier energy. Rate goes to zero as γ → 0 (the "Kramers turnover": rate is non-monotonic in friction, maximal at intermediate γ). Transcribed structure only; do not quote constants.

- TST bound. HTB1990 state that "the transition-state k_TST seriously overestimates the true rate" (fetched quotation) — i.e. Kramers' prefactor is a friction-dependent *correction* to the Arrhenius/TST law k = A·exp(−Eb/kT). The Arrhenius exponential dependence on barrier/temperature is the robust part; the prefactor is where the physics of the bath enters.

**What is rigorous here.** Kramers' formula is an asymptotic result for a one-dimensional Langevin/Fokker–Planck system with additive Gaussian white noise in the limit of high barrier. The mean first-passage time formula from which it derives (Gardiner ch. 5.2.7; Risken ch. 5) is exact for 1-D diffusions:
  τ(x0 → b) = (1/D) ∫_{x0}^{b} dy e^{U(y)/D} ∫_{−∞}^{y} dz e^{−U(z)/D}  (overdamped, reflecting at −∞, absorbing at b) — **textbook-standard (Gardiner), not transcribed this session.**
It is *not* specific to chemistry: any system describable as overdamped motion in a potential with noise (dx = −U'(x)dt + √(2D) dW) obeys it, with "kT" replaced by the noise intensity D. That is the legitimate bridge to a behavioural model — provided one can justify (i) a scalar state, (ii) a potential with a well and a barrier, (iii) additive white noise, (iv) barrier ≫ noise.

### 2. Catastrophe theory and the cusp

**Foundational texts.**
- Thom, R. (1972). *Stabilité structurelle et morphogénèse*. Benjamin/InterÉditions; English: *Structural Stability and Morphogenesis* (1975, Benjamin, transl. D. H. Fowler). — **UNVERIFIED** (standard reference; not fetched). [Thom1975]
- Zeeman, E. C. (1976). "Catastrophe theory." *Scientific American* 234(4), 65–83. — **UNVERIFIED** (volume/pages from memory). Zeeman's dog example: aggression as a cusp with *rage* and *fear* as the two control axes, behaviour (attack ⇄ flight) as the state; sudden jumps and hysteresis between attack and retreat when both drivers are high. Collected in Zeeman, E. C. (1977). *Catastrophe Theory: Selected Papers 1972–1977*. Addison-Wesley — **UNVERIFIED**. [Zeeman1976]

**The cusp model itself (from the fetched JSS paper, Grasman et al. 2009).**
- Potential: **−V(y; α, β) = αy + ½βy² − ¼y⁴** (transcribed).
- Equilibria: **α + βy − y³ = 0** (transcribed). For β > 0 there are up to three roots (bistability; the middle one unstable); for β < 0 one root. α is the "normal" or "asymmetry" factor; β the "bifurcation" or "splitting" factor (transcribed nomenclature).
- Cobb's stochastic cusp: **dY = −(∂V/∂Y) dt + dW(t)**, W(t) white noise of variance σ² (the fetched text renders the sign convention as dY = ∂V/∂Y dt + dW with V defined by the "−V = …" form above; the drift is α + βY − Y³, which is the standard Cobb form).
- Stationary density (transcribed form, Cobb): f(y) = ψ · exp[ (α y + ½β y² − ¼ y⁴) / σ² ] with normalizing constant ψ, after the affine rescaling y → (Y − λ)/scale. This is the Boltzmann-type density exp(−V/σ²) of the overdamped Langevin equation — i.e. **Cobb's cusp regression and Kramers' escape describe the same SDE**: the cusp gives the shape of the double well, Kramers gives the hopping rate between its two minima. This equivalence is the cleanest rigorous link between the two metaphors the treatise already uses.
- Control variables are regressed on covariates: αᵢ = a₀ + a₁X₁ + … , βᵢ = b₀ + b₁X₁ + … (transcribed), and the model is fitted by maximum likelihood on the stationary density; fit is compared to linear and logistic alternatives via AIC/BIC and pseudo-R².
- Citation: Grasman, R., van der Maas, H. L. J., & Wagenmakers, E.-J. (2009). "Fitting the Cusp Catastrophe in R: A cusp Package Primer." *Journal of Statistical Software* 32(8), 1–27. DOI 10.18637/jss.v032.i08 — **VERIFIED** (JSS page fetched). Cited underlying works: Cobb & Ragade (1978), Cobb (1980, 1981), Cobb & Watson (1980), Hartelman (1997), Wagenmakers et al. (2005a, b) — **VERIFIED as cited in Grasman et al.; individual DOIs UNVERIFIED**. The fetched paper mentions Zeeman's exchange-market-crash cusp (Zeeman 1974) and bistable visual perception as canonical applications. [Grasman2009]

**The critique.** Zahler, R. S., & Sussmann, H. J. (1977). "Claims and accomplishments of applied catastrophe theory." *Nature* 269, 759–763 (27 October 1977). DOI 10.1038/269759a0 — **VERIFIED** (Nature page fetched). Abstract, verbatim: "Several representative attempts to apply catastrophe theory to biological and social science problems turn out on close analysis to be characterised by incorrect reasoning, far-fetched assumptions, erroneous consequences, and exaggerated claims." Their central technical objections (from the paper and the follow-up Sussmann & Zahler 1978, *Synthese* 37, 117–216, "Catastrophe theory as applied to the social and biological sciences: a critique" — **UNVERIFIED** DOI): (a) Thom's classification theorem applies to gradient systems with ≤ 4 control parameters; social/behavioural systems are rarely shown to be gradient systems; (b) the "catastrophe flags" (bimodality, hysteresis, sudden jumps, divergence, inaccessibility) can be produced by many non-catastrophe mechanisms, so their presence does not *deduce* a cusp; (c) many applications were purely qualitative curve-drawing with no fitted parameters. Responses: Zeeman and others replied in *Nature* letters in 1977–78 (**UNVERIFIED** — the Nature page fetched did not list them); Guckenheimer's review "The catastrophe controversy" (*Mathematical Intelligencer* 1(1), 15–20, 1978 — **UNVERIFIED**); Sussmann & Zahler's own "On deducing the presence of catastrophes" (*Mathématiques et Sciences Humaines* 64, 71–92, 1978 — **VERIFIED** to exist via EuDML/Numdam search result; content not fetched). The upshot accepted by later applied workers (Guastello, van der Maas): a cusp claim is only credible when it is *statistically fitted* against linear/logistic alternatives and when the control variables are specified a priori — which is exactly what Cobb's method and the `cusp` package were built to supply.

**Guastello's occupational-stress / accident cusps.**
- Guastello, S. J. (1988). "Catastrophe modeling of the accident process: organizational subunit size." *Psychological Bulletin* 103(2), 246–255 — **UNVERIFIED** (title/venue from memory). Fits a cusp with accident rate as the behavioural variable; stress/hazard and subunit size or experience as control variables, using Guastello's polynomial-regression ("difference equation") method: Δz = b0 + b1 z1³ + b2 z1² + b3 a z1 + b4 b, compared with linear pre-post alternatives.
- Guastello, S. J. (2013). "Catastrophe theory and its applications to I/O psychology." In J. M. Cortina & R. S. Landis (eds.), *Modern Research Methods for the Study of Behavior in Organizations*, Routledge — **UNVERIFIED**.
- Guastello, S. J., & Gregson, R. A. M. (eds.) (2011). *Nonlinear Dynamical Systems Analysis for the Behavioral Sciences Using Real Data*. CRC Press — **UNVERIFIED** (the question gives no year; 2011 is the edition I know of). Contains the Guastello polynomial-regression method and the Cobb/Grasman maximum-likelihood method side by side, with chapters on cusp models of occupational accidents, fatigue and workload ("cusp catastrophe model of cognitive workload and fatigue", Guastello et al., 2012–2015, *Nonlinear Dynamics, Psychology, and Life Sciences* — **UNVERIFIED**, but this line of work is the closest published precedent for a cusp of *operator* performance under workload and fatigue).
- Chen, D.-G., & Chen, X. (2017/2015). "Cusp catastrophe modeling in medical and health research." Springer chapter DOI 10.1007/978-3-319-18536-1_12 — **VERIFIED** (Springer page in search results). A review of quantitative cusp-fitting methods (Cobb ML, Guastello regression, Hartelman/van der Maas). [Chen2015]
- van der Maas, H. L. J., & Molenaar, P. C. M. (1992). "Stadium-wise cognitive development: an application of catastrophe theory." *Psychological Review* 99(3), 395–417 — **UNVERIFIED**. Establishes the eight "catastrophe flags" as empirical tests for cusp models in psychology; van der Maas's later work (e.g. Ploeger, van der Maas & Hartelman 2002 on bistable perception) is the psychological canon for cusp fitting.
- Historical review: Stewart & Peregoy (1983), "Catastrophe theory modeling in psychology," *Psychological Bulletin* 94(2), 336–362 — **UNVERIFIED**; and "Catastrophe theory applications in clinical psychology: a review," *Current Psychology* (Springer, DOI 10.1007/BF02686568 — **VERIFIED** to exist via search result; authors not confirmed).

### 3. Drift-diffusion models of decision (Wiener and Ornstein–Uhlenbeck processes)

- Ratcliff, R. (1978). "A theory of memory retrieval." *Psychological Review* 85(2), 59–108 — **UNVERIFIED** (standard; DOI 10.1037/0033-295X.85.2.59 from memory). Introduces the diffusion model: evidence x(t) accumulates as a Wiener process with drift v and diffusion coefficient s, dx = v dt + s dW, between absorbing boundaries 0 and a, starting at z; response time = first-passage time + non-decision time Ter. [Ratcliff1978]
- Ratcliff, R., & McKoon, G. (2008). "The diffusion decision model: theory and data for two-choice decision tasks." *Neural Computation* 20(4), 873–922. DOI 10.1162/neco.2008.12-06-420 — **UNVERIFIED** (DOI from memory; not fetched). The standard review; parameters (drift v, boundary separation a, starting point z, non-decision time Ter, plus trial-to-trial variabilities η, sz, st) and the across-trial predictions for correct/error RT distributions. [RatcliffMcKoon2008]
- Ornstein–Uhlenbeck variants: Busemeyer & Townsend (1993), "Decision field theory," *Psychological Review* 100(3), 432–459 — **UNVERIFIED**; Usher & McClelland (2001), "The time course of perceptual choice: the leaky, competing accumulator model," *Psychological Review* 108(3), 550–592 — **UNVERIFIED**; Bogacz, Brown, Moehlis, Holmes & Cohen (2006), "The physics of optimal decision making," *Psychological Review* 113(4), 700–765 — **UNVERIFIED** (DOI 10.1037/0033-295X.113.4.700 from memory). Bogacz et al. is the key bridge paper: it shows the leaky/OU accumulator dx = (−λx + v)dt + s dW reduces to the pure DDM when λ → 0, relates the DDM to the sequential probability ratio test (Wald), and — importantly for the treatise — derives the *optimal speed–accuracy trade-off* in a reward-rate framework, which is a normative account of why operators shift boundary a under time pressure.
- The **Wiener first-passage-time density** for one absorbing boundary (Ratcliff 1978, Eq. in appendix; also Cox & Miller, *Theory of Stochastic Processes*, 1965): textbook-standard; not transcribed here.

The DDM is the best-validated stochastic-dynamics model of *any* human cognitive process: 45 years of fits to choice RT data, neural correlates in LIP/FEF firing rates (Gold & Shadlen 2007, *Annual Review of Neuroscience* 30, 535–574 — **UNVERIFIED**), and standard software (fast-dm, HDDM, EZ-diffusion — Wagenmakers, van der Maas & Grasman 2007, *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review* 14(1), 3–22 — **UNVERIFIED**). Note the *same authors* (van der Maas, Grasman, Wagenmakers) sit at the intersection of DDM and cusp fitting, which is useful for the treatise's coherence.

### 4. Early-warning signals of critical transitions

- Scheffer, M., Bascompte, J., Brock, W. A., Brovkin, V., Carpenter, S. R., Dakos, V., Held, H., van Nes, E. H., Rietkerk, M., & Sugihara, G. (2009). "Early-warning signals for critical transitions." *Nature* 461, 53–59. DOI 10.1038/nature08227 — **UNVERIFIED** (DOI from memory; not fetched this session, but this is a very widely cited paper). Generic indicators as a system approaches a fold (saddle-node) bifurcation: (i) **critical slowing down** — the dominant eigenvalue of the linearized dynamics → 0, so recovery from perturbation slows; (ii) **rising lag-1 autocorrelation** of fluctuations; (iii) **rising variance**; (iv) **skewness** toward the alternative state; (v) **flickering** — with larger noise the system visits both basins before the bifurcation, giving bimodality. Caveats stated by the authors: the indicators are necessary-not-sufficient, need long stationary time series, and can be produced by other mechanisms. [Scheffer2009]
- Companion methods: Dakos et al. (2012), "Methods for detecting early warnings of critical transitions in time series illustrated using simulated ecological data," *PLoS ONE* 7(7), e41010 — **UNVERIFIED**; Scheffer et al. (2012), "Anticipating critical transitions," *Science* 338, 344–348 — **UNVERIFIED**.
- **Application to psychology/psychiatry.** van de Leemput, I. A., Wichers, M., Cramer, A. O. J., Borsboom, D., Tuerlinckx, F., Kuppens, P., van Nes, E. H., Viechtbauer, W., Giltay, E. J., Aggen, S. H., Derom, C., Jacobs, N., Kendler, K. S., van der Maas, H. L. J., Neale, M. C., Peeters, F., Thiery, E., Zachar, P., & Scheffer, M. (2014). "Critical slowing down as early warning for the onset and termination of depression." *PNAS* 111(1), 87–92. DOI 10.1073/pnas.1312114110 — **DOI VERIFIED** (returned in PNAS search-result URL and PMC3890822); **content not fetched** (403/CAPTCHA), so the following is from memory: using experience-sampling (momentary mood ratings several times per day over days–weeks) in two large cohorts, elevated temporal autocorrelation, variance and cross-correlation of emotions (e.g. "cheerful", "content", "sad", "anxious") were higher in individuals who later transitioned into or out of depression. Interpretation: mood is a bistable system near a tipping point. [vdLeemput2014]
- Wichers, M., Groot, P. C., & Psychosystems/ESM Group (2016). "Critical slowing down as a personalized early warning signal for depression." *Psychotherapy and Psychosomatics* 85(2), 114–116 — **VERIFIED** to exist (Karger page in search results; DOI 10.1159/000441458 **UNVERIFIED**). A single-case (n=1) time series of ~250 days of ESM data during antidepressant tapering showing rising autocorrelation and variance before a depressive relapse. Also Wichers & Groot (2016) and the later "Elevated emotion network connectivity is associated with fluctuations in depression," *PNAS* (DOI 10.1073/pnas.2216499120 — **VERIFIED** to exist via search result). [Wichers2016]
- Physics framing: Kuehn, C. (2011), "A mathematical framework for critical transitions: bifurcations, fast–slow systems and stochastic dynamics," *Physica D* 240(12), 1020–1035 — **UNVERIFIED**. Makes the connection explicit: for an overdamped SDE approaching a fold, variance ∝ 1/|λ| and AC(1) ≈ exp(λΔt) → 1 as λ → 0⁻. For the treatise this is the mathematical thread that ties Kramers (hopping between wells), the cusp (fold lines of the cusp are exactly the saddle-node bifurcations where slowing down occurs) and early-warning signals into one object.

### 7. Does any published model apply Kramers/Arrhenius rates to human cognition or fatigue?

Searched: "Kramers" + cognitive / decision / attention lapse / drowsiness / double-well. Result of the honest search: **no paper was found that fits a Kramers escape rate to human operator state, fatigue or attentional lapses.** What does exist:

- **Double-well / Kramers in neuroscience of perception and decision.** Bistable perception (binocular rivalry, Necker cube) is modelled as noise-driven hopping between two wells, and dominance-duration distributions are compared with Kramers-type first-passage statistics: Moreno-Bote, Rinzel & Rubin (2007), "Noise-induced alternations in an attractor network model of perceptual bistability," *Journal of Neurophysiology* 98(3), 1125–1139 — **UNVERIFIED**; Braun & Mattia (2010), "Attractors and noise: twin drivers of decisions and multistability," *NeuroImage* 52(3), 740–751 — **UNVERIFIED**. In attractor models of decision (Wang 2002, *Neuron* 36, 955–968 — **UNVERIFIED**; Roxin & Ledberg 2008, "Neurobiological models of two-choice decision making can be reduced to a one-dimensional nonlinear diffusion equation," *PLoS Computational Biology* 4(3), e1000046 — **UNVERIFIED**), the reduction to a 1-D normal-form diffusion in a double-well is explicit, and escape from the "undecided" saddle is Kramers-like. These are the strongest published precedents for saying "Kramers describes a cognitive transition" — but at the level of *neural population dynamics in a single decision*, not of an operator's shift-scale collapse.
- **Two-state / Markov models of attentional lapses.** The DDM literature models lapses as a mixture: on a fraction p of trials the process is a "guess" or the drift is zero (Ratcliff & Van Dongen 2011, "Diffusion model for one-choice reaction-time tasks and the cognitive effects of sleep deprivation," *PNAS* 108(27), 11285–11290 — **UNVERIFIED**; this is the closest to "fatigue → stochastic-dynamics parameter" — sleep deprivation lowers drift v and raises its variability, producing the long-RT tail of the Psychomotor Vigilance Task). Hidden-Markov-model treatments of "in-the-zone / out-of-the-zone" attention states (Esterman, Noonan, Rosenberg & DeGutis 2013, "In the zone or zoning out? Tracking behavioral and neural fluctuations during sustained attention," *Cerebral Cortex* 23(11), 2712–2723 — **UNVERIFIED**) and RT-variability triggering (Shelat, Schooler & Giesbrecht 2024, "Predicting attentional lapses using response time speed in continuous performance tasks," *Frontiers in Cognition* 3, 1460349, DOI 10.3389/fcogn.2024.1460349 — **VERIFIED**, fetched; it explicitly does **not** use Kramers, double-well or Arrhenius models). [Shelat2024]
- **Drowsiness/sleep transitions.** The wake–sleep switch is modelled as a bistable "flip-flop" (Saper, Scammell & Lu 2005, *Nature* 437, 1257–1263 — **UNVERIFIED**) and in physiologically based models (Phillips & Robinson 2007, "A quantitative model of sleep-wake dynamics based on the physiology of the brainstem ascending arousal system," *Journal of Biological Rhythms* 22(2), 167–179 — **UNVERIFIED**) that exhibit a fold/hysteresis structure formally identical to a cusp; noise-driven state transitions in these models are Kramers-type, though the authors do not usually cite Kramers. Microsleep/lapse frequency under sleep pressure is modelled empirically (Two-Process model, Borbély 1982; the "sleep/wake predictor" and SAFTE/FAST fatigue models — **UNVERIFIED**), not with escape-rate formulas.
- **Arrhenius-type rates in psychology.** Not found as a modelling device. The "temperature" concept appears in softmax/Boltzmann choice rules (Luce choice, reinforcement learning β) — exp(Q/τ) — which is a Gibbs distribution over options, not a barrier-crossing rate; conflating the two would be an error.

**Conclusion for §7:** Kramers escape is rigorously established for (a) overdamped 1-D SDEs in general, (b) cusp-type stochastic models (Cobb), (c) neural attractor models of single decisions and bistable perception. It is **not** an established model of operator fatigue, workload collapse or attentional lapses at the time-scale of a shift. Any use for control-room operators is an *analogy with a well-defined mathematical content* (the SDE), not a citation to prior human-factors work.

## What an engineering treatise can legitimately say

- **Established for humans (cite freely):** the drift-diffusion model for speeded choice (Ratcliff 1978; Ratcliff & McKoon 2008; Bogacz et al. 2006), including its use to quantify sleep-deprivation and vigilance effects (Ratcliff & Van Dongen 2011). This is the only stochastic-dynamics model in the list with decades of quantitative fits to human data and standard parameter-estimation software.
- **Established as a statistical model class, contested as a theory:** the cusp catastrophe. It is legitimate to say "cusp models have been fitted to occupational accident, workload and fatigue data (Guastello) and to psychological data (van der Maas; Grasman et al. 2009)", provided the treatise (i) cites Zahler & Sussmann 1977 and (ii) states the modern standard: a cusp is only asserted after maximum-likelihood or polynomial-regression fitting beats linear/logistic alternatives on the same data (`cusp` package, Guastello method).
- **Kramers/Arrhenius is a mathematical fact about the SDE, not a human-factors finding.** The honest formulation: "If operator state is represented by a scalar y obeying dy = (α + βy − y³)dt + σ dW (the stochastic cusp), then the expected time to a spontaneous jump between the two stable states is given by Kramers' formula with barrier ΔV = V(saddle) − V(well) and 'temperature' σ²/2, valid when ΔV ≫ σ²/2." Say *if*; do not say that anyone has measured ω0, ωb, γ or ΔU for a human operator, because nobody has.
- **Early-warning signals are a legitimate, published cross-domain method** (Scheffer et al. 2009), *already* applied to human mood time series (van de Leemput et al. 2014; Wichers 2016). A treatise may propose rising AC(1), variance and flickering in operator metrics (RT, alarm-acknowledge latency, HRV) as precursors of collapse, citing these — but must carry Scheffer's caveats (not sufficient; needs long stationary series; false positives from non-critical mechanisms).
- **Yerkes–Dodson should be cited only as history**, with Teigen 1994 and Corbett 2015 as the corrective, and Hancock & Warm 1989 as the human-factors replacement (extended-U / adaptive-range model). Do not draw "the" inverted-U as if it were in the 1908 paper.
- **Kuramoto is an analogy** for team coherence. The empirical literature (Elkins et al. 2009; Gorman; Kazi et al. 2021 review) uses correlation/coherence/recurrence measures, has mixed results, and does not fit coupling constants. Say "an order-parameter-like index of team synchrony has been proposed," not "teams obey the Kuramoto model."
- **To fit any of these to control-room data one would need:** (a) a defined scalar state variable sampled densely and stationarily (e.g. RT on secondary task, alarm-response latency, HRV/EDA at ≥ 1 Hz), for hundreds of transitions per operator to fit a cusp by ML or estimate an escape rate; (b) a priori control variables (workload/alarm rate as β, time-on-task or sleep pressure as α) rather than post-hoc ones, per Zahler & Sussmann; (c) demonstration of bimodality and hysteresis in the *same* data (van der Maas's flags); (d) for early-warning signals, rolling-window AC(1)/variance with detrending and surrogate-data significance tests (Dakos et al. 2012); (e) for a Kramers rate, an independent estimate of noise intensity σ² (from the residual fluctuations in the stable regime) and of the barrier from the fitted cusp potential — then a comparison of predicted vs observed mean time-to-jump. Absent (a)–(e), the models are illustrative.
