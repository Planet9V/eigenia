# q9 — Legal and ethical constraints on inferring/displaying employee psychological state in the EU/NL, and prior work on sonifying physiological, affective and social state

Researched 2026-09-11. Primary legal texts were downloaded from EUR-Lex (CELEX 32024R1689, 32016R0679), wetten.overheid.nl and ec.europa.eu and quoted verbatim; everything marked VERIFIED below was read in the source itself. Items marked UNVERIFIED are recalled from background knowledge and were not confirmed in this session.

## Findings

### 1. EU AI Act — Regulation (EU) 2024/1689

**1.1 The prohibition.** Article 5(1) opens "The following AI practices shall be prohibited:" and point (f) reads, verbatim (VERIFIED, EUR-Lex OJ L 2024/1689):

> "(f) the placing on the market, the putting into service for this specific purpose, or the use of AI systems to infer emotions of a natural person in the areas of workplace and education institutions, except where the use of the AI system is intended to be put in place or into the market for medical or safety reasons;"

**1.2 Definitions (Art. 3), verbatim (VERIFIED):**

> "(39) 'emotion recognition system' means an AI system for the purpose of identifying or inferring emotions or intentions of natural persons on the basis of their biometric data;"

> "(34) 'biometric data' means personal data resulting from specific technical processing relating to the physical, physiological or behavioural characteristics of a natural person, such as facial images or dactyloscopic data;"

Note the AI Act definition of biometric data deliberately drops the GDPR's "which allow or confirm the unique identification" qualifier (the Commission Guidelines, fn. 160, say so explicitly). So HRV, EEG, ECG, voice, keystroke dynamics and eye tracking are "biometric data" for AI Act purposes even when they cannot identify anyone.

> "(1) 'AI system' means a machine-based system that is designed to operate with varying levels of autonomy and that may exhibit adaptiveness after deployment, and that, for explicit or implicit objectives, infers, from the input it receives, how to generate outputs such as predictions, content, recommendations, or decisions that can influence physical or virtual environments;"

**1.3 Recital 18 (VERIFIED, verbatim):**

> "(18) The notion of 'emotion recognition system' referred to in this Regulation should be defined as an AI system for the purpose of identifying or inferring emotions or intentions of natural persons on the basis of their biometric data. The notion refers to emotions or intentions such as happiness, sadness, anger, surprise, disgust, embarrassment, excitement, shame, contempt, satisfaction and amusement. It does not include physical states, such as pain or fatigue, including, for example, systems used in detecting the state of fatigue of professional pilots or drivers for the purpose of preventing accidents. This does also not include the mere detection of readily apparent expressions, gestures or movements, unless they are used for identifying or inferring emotions. Those expressions can be basic facial expressions, such as a frown or a smile, or gestures such as the movement of hands, arms or head, or characteristics of a person's voice, such as a raised voice or whispering."

**1.4 Recital 44 (VERIFIED, verbatim):**

> "(44) There are serious concerns about the scientific basis of AI systems aiming to identify or infer emotions, particularly as expression of emotions vary considerably across cultures and situations, and even within a single individual. Among the key shortcomings of such systems are the limited reliability, the lack of specificity and the limited generalisability. Therefore, AI systems identifying or inferring emotions or intentions of natural persons on the basis of their biometric data may lead to discriminatory outcomes and can be intrusive to the rights and freedoms of the concerned persons. Considering the imbalance of power in the context of work or education, combined with the intrusive nature of these systems, such systems could lead to detrimental or unfavourable treatment of certain natural persons or whole groups thereof. Therefore, the placing on the market, the putting into service, or the use of AI systems intended to be used to detect the emotional state of individuals in situations related to the workplace and education should be prohibited. That prohibition should not cover AI systems placed on the market strictly for medical or safety reasons, such as systems intended for therapeutical use."

The legislator's stated reason is the same scientific point the treatise series already relies on (Barrett et al. 2019): the Commission Guidelines (fn. 150) cite Feldman Barrett et al., "Emotional Expressions Reconsidered", Psychological Science in the Public Interest 2019, as the basis for the "serious concerns".

**1.5 Date of application (VERIFIED).** Article 113: "It shall apply from 2 August 2026. However: (a) Chapters I and II shall apply from 2 February 2025; ..." Article 5 is in Chapter II, so the prohibition has applied since 2 February 2025. Fines for Art. 5 breaches (Art. 99(3)) are up to EUR 35 million or 7 % of worldwide annual turnover (UNVERIFIED figure — not re-read this session).

**1.6 Transparency for permitted emotion recognition (VERIFIED).** Art. 50(3): "Deployers of an emotion recognition system or a biometric categorisation system shall inform the natural persons exposed thereto of the operation of the system, and shall process the personal data in accordance with Regulations (EU) 2016/679 and (EU) 2018/1725 and Directive (EU) 2016/680, as applicable."

**1.7 High-risk classification (Annex III, VERIFIED verbatim):**

> "1. Biometrics, in so far as their use is permitted under relevant Union or national law: ... (c) AI systems intended to be used for emotion recognition."

> "4. Employment, workers' management and access to self-employment: (a) AI systems intended to be used for the recruitment or selection of natural persons ...; (b) AI systems intended to be used to make decisions affecting terms of work-related relationships, the promotion or termination of work-related contractual relationships, to allocate tasks based on individual behaviour or personal traits or characteristics or to monitor and evaluate the performance and behaviour of persons in such relationships."

Also relevant: Annex III point 2 "Critical infrastructure: AI systems intended to be used as safety components in the management and operation of critical digital infrastructure, road traffic, or in the supply of water, gas, heating or electricity" — a control-room decision aid that becomes a safety component is high-risk on that ground alone. Art. 26(7) (VERIFIED) requires deployers of high-risk systems in the workplace to "inform workers' representatives and the affected workers that they will be subject to the use of the high-risk AI system". High-risk obligations apply from 2 August 2026 (Art. 113).

**1.8 Commission Guidelines on prohibited practices.** The Commission approved draft guidelines on 4 February 2025 (C(2025) 884 final) and formally adopted them as a Communication on 29 July 2025 (C(2025) 5052 final). The adopted text was downloaded from ec.europa.eu and read (VERIFIED); paragraph numbers below are from the adopted version. Section 7 ("Article 5(1)(f) AI Act emotion recognition") says:

- Scope alignment (paras 244-245): although Art. 5(1)(f) says "AI systems to infer emotions" rather than "emotion recognition systems", the prohibition should be limited "to inferences based on a person's biometric data" and covers both identifying and inferring emotions or intentions.
- Emotions read broadly (para 247): "the concept of emotions or intentions should be understood in a wide sense and not interpreted restrictively"; the Recital 18 list is "not exhaustive". Anti-circumvention (para 248): "The prohibition should not be circumvented by referring to attitudes".
- Physical states (para 249, and 265): fatigue and pain are out of scope; "Identifying whether a person is sick is not emotion recognition"; "Using AI recognition systems to infer a professional pilot's or driver's fatigue to alert them and suggest when to take brakes to avoid accidents is not 'emotion recognition'". But "Concluding that a person is happy is emotion recognition."
- Biometric inputs (para 251): explicitly lists "eye tracking and heartbeats, electroencephalography (EEG), or electrocardiograms (ECG)" as behavioural-biometric modalities; "An AI system inferring emotions from key stroke (way of typing), facial expressions, body postures or movements is based on biometric data and falls within the scope of the prohibition." Text/sentiment analysis is out of scope.
- Workplace (para 254): "The notion of 'workplace' should be interpreted broadly. That notion relates to any specific physical or virtual space where natural persons engage in tasks and responsibilities assigned by their employer ... spanning from indoor office spaces, factories and warehouses to publicly accessible spaces ... to open-air sites or cars, as well as temporary or mobile work sites." A control room is plainly a workplace.
- Team-level inference is expressly caught (para 254 example): "AI systems monitoring the emotional tone in hybrid work teams by identifying and inferring emotions from voice and imagery of hybrid video calls, which would typically serve the purpose of fostering social awareness, emotional dynamics management, and conflict prevention, are prohibited." Also: "Using webcams and voice recognition systems by a call centre to track their employee's emotions, such as anger, is prohibited." Emotion recognition used purely for personal training is tolerated only "if the results are not shared with HR responsible persons and cannot impact the assessment, promotion etc. of the person trained".
- "Arousal" is treated as an emotion (para 255 examples): eye-tracking for exam proctoring is fine, but "if the system is also used to detect emotions, such as emotional arousal and anxiousness, this would fall within the scope of the prohibition." This matters directly for any system whose output is framed as "arousal".
- The medical/safety exception is narrow (paras 256-263): "this exception should be narrowly interpreted"; "therapeutic uses should be understood to mean uses of CE-marked medical devices"; "The general monitoring of stress levels at the workplace is not permitted under health or safety aspects. For example, an AI system intended to detect burnout or depression at the workplace ... would remain prohibited." Safety means "only ... the protection of life and health". Use must be "limited to what is strictly necessary and proportionate, including limits in time, personal application and scale" with safeguards such as "prior written and motivated expert opinion", and necessity "should not refer to the employer's ... 'needs'". Worked example (para 263): "An employer would be prohibited from deploying AI-enabled devices or digital assistants at the workplace for measuring anxiety based on measured stress levels or for measuring boredom of employees, unless the elevated stress level/lack of concentration would pose a specific danger, for example when deploying dangerous machines or dealing with dangerous chemicals. In the latter case, the employer may not use the data for other purposes, such as assessing the employee's work performance." Footnote 167 adds that employers must also consult workers' representatives under national procedures and that data-protection consent rules remain applicable; para 260 notes "Continuous monitoring via wearables, for instance, may increase work-stress while affecting productivity."
- Aggregate/crowd systems (paras 267-270): a crowd-control system "analysing the general noise and mood level at a given place" does not infer emotions "of (a concrete) natural person" and is out of scope — but this reasoning is applied to public spaces, and para 270 says that where such systems incidentally screen staff, "deployers must employ safeguards to avoid the screening of employees". A small control-room crew is not a crowd; averaging four operators' inferred emotions still infers each operator's emotions first.
- Other law continues to apply (para 262) and Member States may adopt stricter worker-protective rules (para 264, Art. 2(11)).

**1.9 Commission Guidelines on the definition of an AI system** (C(2025) 5053 final, 29 July 2025, downloaded and read — VERIFIED). Para 40 (quoting Recital 12): the definition "should not cover systems that are based on the rules defined solely by natural persons to automatically execute operations." Para 46: "Basic data processing system refers to a system that follows predefined, explicit instructions or operations ... without any 'learning, reasoning or modelling'". Para 47: "systems that solely intended for descriptive analysis, hypothesis testing, and visualisation, fall outside the definition of an AI system" — the example is a sales dashboard that "simply present[s] data in an informative way" but "does not recommend how to improve sales". A fixed, human-authored parameter-mapping sonification of process/interaction data is arguably a "visualisation" system outside the AI Act; a learned model that infers a latent psychological state from biometric input is not.

### 2. GDPR (Regulation (EU) 2016/679) and Dutch implementation

**2.1 Special categories (Art. 9(1), VERIFIED verbatim):** "Processing of personal data revealing racial or ethnic origin, political opinions, religious or philosophical beliefs, or trade union membership, and the processing of genetic data, biometric data for the purpose of uniquely identifying a natural person, data concerning health or data concerning a natural person's sex life or sexual orientation shall be prohibited." Art. 4(15): "'data concerning health' means personal data related to the physical or mental health of a natural person ... which reveal information about his or her health status." Recital 35 extends this to "any information on ... the physiological or biomedical state of the data subject independent of its source". Art. 4(14) biometric data under the GDPR requires that the data "allow or confirm the unique identification" — so HRV/EDA are generally *not* GDPR-biometric, but they are very likely *health data* (physiological state; mental-health inferences such as stress, anxiety, burnout risk fall under "mental health"). Psychometric inferences about mood/affect are health data when they reveal mental-health status; a mere workload index derived from plant/interaction data probably is not, but the line is thin and the Dutch regulator draws it strictly (see 2.4).

**2.2 Art. 9(2)(b) employment ground and Dutch UAVG Art. 30 (VERIFIED, wetten.overheid.nl).** The Dutch implementation law (Uitvoeringswet AVG) lifts the health-data ban for employers only "voor zover de verwerking noodzakelijk is voor: a. een goede uitvoering van wettelijke voorschriften, pensioenregelingen of collectieve arbeidsovereenkomsten die voorzien in aanspraken die afhankelijk zijn van de gezondheidstoestand van de betrokkene; of b. de re-integratie of begeleiding van werknemers of uitkeringsgerechtigden in verband met ziekte of arbeidsongeschiktheid." Continuous physiological monitoring of healthy operators fits neither limb. Explicit consent (Art. 9(2)(a)) is the only other candidate and is widely regarded as invalid in employment (2.4). Art. 9(2)(h) (occupational medicine) requires processing "by or under the responsibility of a professional subject to the obligation of professional secrecy" — i.e. an occupational physician, not a shift supervisor.

**2.3 Art. 22 (VERIFIED verbatim):** "The data subject shall have the right not to be subject to a decision based solely on automated processing, including profiling, which produces legal effects concerning him or her or similarly significantly affects him or her." Art. 22(4): such decisions "shall not be based on special categories of personal data referred to in Article 9(1), unless point (a) or (g) of Article 9(2) applies". A supervisor acting on a sonified state-signal is human-in-the-loop, so Art. 22 is engaged only if the system itself triggers consequences (e.g. automatic task reallocation, removal from position).

**2.4 Art. 88 (VERIFIED verbatim):** Member States may adopt more specific employment rules; para 2 requires "suitable and specific measures to safeguard the data subject's human dignity, legitimate interests and fundamental rights, with particular regard to the transparency of processing ... and monitoring systems at the work place." The Netherlands has not enacted a dedicated Art. 88 statute; the practical regime is GDPR + UAVG + WOR + Autoriteit Persoonsgegevens (AP) guidance.

**2.5 Article 29 Working Party Opinion 2/2017 on data processing at work (WP 249, adopted 8 June 2017; endorsed by the EDPB) — VERIFIED, downloaded from ec.europa.eu.** Section 5.4.4 "Wearable devices": employer-provided wearables that "track and monitor their health and activity" involve "the processing of health data"; "Given the unequal relationship between employers and employees ... it is highly unlikely that legally valid explicit consent can be given"; "Even if the employer uses a third party to collect the health data, which would only provide aggregated information about general health developments to the employer, the processing would still be unlawful"; and complete anonymisation is "technically very difficult". The worked example concerns devices that "register their heartbeats and sleeping patterns over time": "The resulting health data should only be accessible to the employee and not the employer." No EDPB opinion specifically on emotion recognition was found in this session; the EDPB Guidelines 3/2019 on video devices and Guidelines 05/2022 on facial recognition in law enforcement exist but were not read here (UNVERIFIED).

**2.6 Autoriteit Persoonsgegevens (AP).** (a) In March 2016 the AP forced two Dutch employers to stop processing employee wearable data (movement, sleep) because "gegevens over de hoeveelheid beweging en gegevens over slaappatronen zijn gevoelige persoonsgegevens die iets zeggen over de gezondheid ... In een arbeidsverhouding, waarin de werknemer financieel afhankelijk is van de werkgever, is over het algemeen geen sprake van 'vrije' toestemming" — and this applies also to colleagues or the vendor processing on the employer's behalf, "ook niet als dit anoniem gebeurt" (VERIFIED via NJB news report of 15 March 2016 and CMS client note of 20 April 2016; AP's own page returned HTTP 403). (b) The AP is the designated coordinating supervisor for the AI Act prohibitions in the Netherlands and ran a public call for input on "AI-systemen voor emotieherkenning op de werkplek of in het onderwijs", publishing a summary of responses and next steps in February 2025 (document URL found on autoriteitpersoonsgegevens.nl — existence VERIFIED, content not readable in this session: UNVERIFIED). (c) The AP's general conditions for employee monitoring (legitimate interest, necessity/proportionality, DPIA for systematic monitoring, prior works-council consent, information to staff, no secret monitoring except under strict conditions) are published on its site but the page could not be fetched (403): UNVERIFIED as to exact wording.

**2.7 Works Council Act (Wet op de ondernemingsraden, WOR) Art. 27 — VERIFIED verbatim (wetten.overheid.nl, text in force 18-02-2023 onward).** "1. De ondernemer behoeft de instemming van de ondernemingsraad voor elk door hem voorgenomen besluit tot vaststelling, wijziging of intrekking van: ... k. een regeling omtrent het verwerken van alsmede de bescherming van de persoonsgegevens van de in de onderneming werkzame personen; l. een regeling inzake voorzieningen die gericht zijn op of geschikt zijn voor waarneming van of controle op aanwezigheid, gedrag of prestaties van de in de onderneming werkzame personen; ... een en ander voor zover betrekking hebbende op alle of een groep van de in de onderneming werkzame personen." Sub (l) catches any facility "aimed at or suitable for" observing or checking behaviour or performance — so even a non-AI, non-biometric sonification of operator interaction data needs prior works-council consent. Art. 27(4): without consent the employer may seek leave from the kantonrechter, granted only if the refusal is unreasonable or there are "zwaarwegende bedrijfsorganisatorische, bedrijfseconomische of bedrijfssociale redenen". Art. 27(5) (not quoted) makes a decision taken without consent voidable.

### 4. Prior technical work sonifying physiological, affective or social state

- **EEG sonification.** Hermann, Baier, Stephani & Ritter (2006/2008) developed vocal and event-based sonifications of EEG for epilepsy analysis ("Vocal sonification of pathologic EEG features", ICAD 2006 — VERIFIED as a Georgia Tech ICAD repository record; Hermann et al. 2008 kernel-regression sonification cited in Loui et al. 2014). Hinterberger & Baier (2005) "parametric orchestral sonification" of six EEG bands in real time (cited in Loui et al. 2014 — VERIFIED as citation). Väljamäe et al. (2013) "A review of real-time EEG sonification research" (ICAD 2013 — VERIFIED as citation). Loui, Koplin-Green, Frick & Massone (2014) "Rapidly Learned Identification of Epileptic Seizures from Sonified EEG", Front. Hum. Neurosci. 8:820 (VERIFIED, read): naive listeners were at chance before training and significantly above chance after a short training session. Lesson: sonified physiological streams are interpretable, but only after training and only for gross discrete events (seizure vs. non-seizure), not for graded affect.
- **Movement/EMG/rehab and sport.** Vogt, Pirrò, Kobenz, Höldrich & Eckel (2009) "PhysioSonic — Movement sonification as auditory feedback", ICAD 2009 (VERIFIED, read): real-time full-body tracking mapped to sound for shoulder physiotherapy; principles "a simple mapping of movement to sound and common sense metaphors". It cites Pauletto & Hunt (2006) "The sonification of EMG data" (ICAD 2006), Effenberg (2006) on movement sonification in sport, and Hermann, Höner & Ritter's AcouMotion (VERIFIED as citations).
- **Group/team dynamics sonification.** Höner, Hermann & Grunow (2004) "Sonification of Group Behavior for Analysis and Training of Sports Tactics", Proc. Int. Workshop on Interactive Sonification, Bielefeld 2004 (VERIFIED as a citation in Vogt et al. 2009; not read). This is the closest technical precedent to sonifying "team state": it sonified the positional dynamics of players, i.e. observable behaviour, not inferred affect.
- **GSR/EDA and heart-rate sonification.** Numerous ICAD/NIME-era works sonify skin conductance and heart rate for biofeedback and artistic purposes (e.g. "Sonic Cradle", breathing-driven; HRV sonification for relaxation); none was fetched this session (UNVERIFIED). The Commission Guidelines (para 251, fn. 159) themselves cite Hasnul et al. (2021) "Electrocardiogram-Based Emotion Recognition Systems and Their Applications in Healthcare" — evidence that regulators regard ECG-based affect inference as squarely within "emotion recognition".
- **Social signals / sociometric badges.** Kim, Chang, Holland & Pentland (2008) "Meeting Mediator: Enhancing Group Collaboration and Leadership with Sociometric Feedback", CSCW 2008 (VERIFIED, MIT Media Lab abstract): real-time *visual* feedback on mobile phones of speaking-time balance and interactivity captured by sociometric badges; "MM had a significant effect on overlapping speaking time and interactivity level"; badges "were also able to detect dominant players". Pentland's "Honest Signals" (2008) and the commercialisation (Sociometric Solutions/Humanyze) drew sustained criticism as workplace surveillance (the JASSS review by Dugdale 2009 is a documented sceptical academic reception — VERIFIED existence, content not read; the broader "people analytics" ethics controversy is UNVERIFIED here). Note that the Meeting Mediator fed the signal back to the participants themselves, not to a supervisor, and used turn-taking behaviour, not affect.

### 5. Precedent for a supervisor-facing "team state" display in safety-critical industry

- **Bio-mathematical fatigue models (SAFTE-FAST etc.).** Roma, Hursh, Mead & Nesthus (2012), FAA report DOT/FAA/AM-12/12 (VERIFIED, read): the SAFTE model "integrating classical physiological and circadian processes with task effectiveness predictions"; inputs are "Individual sleep, wake, and work patterns" (actigraphy plus duty logs) entered into FAST, output is "Predicted Effectiveness (PVT Speed ... expressed as a % of individual optimum baseline)"; validation against 10,659 PVT sessions from 178 cabin crew gave R² = 0.884 for binned predictions. Key design facts: (i) the data are schedules and sleep/wake, not live biometrics and not affect; (ii) the output is a performance-capacity construct (effectiveness), i.e. a "physical state" (fatigue) that Recital 18 explicitly carves out; (iii) it is population-level.
- **CASA (2010/2014) Biomathematical Fatigue Models Guidance** (VERIFIED via IATA's condensed summary): BFMs are "an optional tool (not a requirement)"; they "tend to be based on averaged fatigue ratings and are restricted therefore to predicting risk probabilities for a target population average rather than the instantaneous fatigue levels of a specific individual"; they "should not be used in isolation or as a go/no go criteria"; their outputs "can give the illusion of being precise and quantitative despite the fact that they simply predict a qualitative measure". Regulators thus deliberately keep the score advisory, schedule-based and non-diagnostic.
- **Rail.** FRA final rule "Fatigue Risk Management Programs for Certain Passenger and Freight Railroads", 87 FR 35660, 13 June 2022 (VERIFIED metadata via Federal Register API) requires FRMPs as part of risk-reduction programmes; it does not mandate physiological monitoring. ICAO Doc 9966 (Manual for the Oversight of Fatigue Management Approaches) and FAA AC 120-103A frame FRMS similarly (UNVERIFIED — not read).
- **Aviation CRM / nuclear crew performance.** CRM (NOTECHS/LOSA-style behavioural markers) and NRC crew-performance evaluation (e.g. NUREG-0711 human-factors review; simulator-based crew assessment) rely on trained human observers rating observable team behaviours (communication, workload management, situation awareness) during training and evaluations — not on continuous sensing of affect displayed live to a supervisor (UNVERIFIED — not fetched). No operational safety-critical system was found that continuously infers and displays a *team's psychological/emotional state* to a supervisor. The nearest live-operations analogues are (a) fatigue-effectiveness scores in FRMS scheduling tools and (b) workload/task-load indices derived from system events, both of which avoid emotion inference by construction.

## What an engineering treatise can legitimately say

- **Do not describe the MPN as inferring emotions, mood, affect, anxiety, stress or "emotional arousal" of operators.** If the system does that from any biometric input (voice, face, HRV, EDA, EEG, keystroke, gaze) in a control room, it is a prohibited practice under Art. 5(1)(f) since 2 February 2025; the Commission reads "emotions" broadly, treats "emotional arousal" and "stress-based anxiety" as emotions, and expressly lists team-level "emotional tone" monitoring as prohibited. Averaging over the crew does not help, because each operator's emotion is inferred first.
- **The legally safe construct is task/system state, not person state.** Sonify plant dynamics, alarm rates, interaction tempo, queue lengths, communication turn-taking counts, or a workload index computed from process and interaction logs. These are not biometric data; if the mapping is a fixed human-authored rule set with no learning, it is arguably not even an "AI system" (Guidelines C(2025) 5053 paras 40-47). Höner/Hermann/Grunow (2004) and the Meeting Mediator show that group *behaviour* can be sonified/visualised without affect inference.
- **Fatigue is the one person-state with a regulatory precedent.** Recital 18 and Guidelines para 249/261 carve out "physical states, such as pain or fatigue", and SAFTE-FAST-type models show the accepted pattern: schedule/sleep inputs, population-level "effectiveness" output, advisory only, never a go/no-go criterion. If the treatise wants a human-state channel, model fatigue/alertness from rosters and self-reports, not from sensors, and keep it advisory.
- **Any biometric or physiological channel must clear GDPR Art. 9.** HRV/EDA/EEG about operators are health data (Recital 35 "physiological or biomedical state"); the AP (2016) and WP 249 §5.4.4 hold that employee consent is not free, that vendor-side or aggregated processing does not cure it, and that "anonymous" reporting to the employer is still unlawful. UAVG Art. 30 offers no employer ground for healthy-worker monitoring. The only defensible route is occupational-medicine processing under Art. 9(2)(h) by an occupational physician, with nothing reaching the shift supervisor — which defeats a supervisor-facing display.
- **Even a non-biometric, non-AI sonification of operator behaviour is a "personeelsvolgsysteem".** WOR Art. 27(1)(k) and (l) require prior works-council consent for any facility "suitable for" observing behaviour or performance; GDPR requires a DPIA (systematic monitoring), a legitimate-interest balancing test, transparency to staff, purpose limitation (no use for appraisal), and strict retention limits. Design in: no per-person identification in the audio stream, no logging of person-attributed state, no export to HR systems.
- **If the MPN is an AI system used in a control room, Annex III high-risk status is likely** either via point 2 (safety component in energy/water/gas supply) or point 4(b) (monitoring performance and behaviour of workers) — with obligations (risk management, human oversight, logging, worker information under Art. 26(7)) applying from 2 August 2026. The treatise should say the system is designed *not* to make or recommend decisions about individuals, only to render situational information.
- **Cite the scientific and legal positions together.** The AI Act's own rationale (Recital 44) and the Commission's footnote to Barrett et al. (2019) mean the treatise can present its refusal to infer emotion as both scientifically and legally principled: physiological signals index arousal/load, and the law forbids re-labelling those as emotions at work.
- **Frame the "psychological state" language as theoretical, not operational.** The treatise series' Lacanian/psychoanalytic vocabulary is legitimate as an interpretive frame for team dynamics; an engineering chapter should explicitly separate that frame from the implemented signal chain, and state that no system output claims to identify a natural person's emotion or intention.

## Gaps

- The AP's February 2025 summary of the emotion-recognition consultation and its employee-monitoring conditions page could not be fetched (HTTP 403); their exact positions are UNVERIFIED and should be read directly before citation.
- ISO 45003:2021 body text is paywalled; no clause-level claims were verified.
- No EDPB opinion specifically addressing emotion recognition in employment was located; WP 249 (2017) is the operative EU-level guidance on wearables at work.
- The GSR/HRV sonification literature (ICAD/NIME) and Effenberg's sport-sonification work were identified only through citations, not read.
- No peer-reviewed or regulatory source was found describing a live "team psychological state" display to supervisors in aviation, rail or nuclear operations; the absence is itself a finding but has not been proven by exhaustive search.
- Whether a fixed-rule sonification of interaction logs escapes the AI Act's "AI system" definition is an arguable position based on Guidelines C(2025) 5053 paras 40-47; it has not been tested by any authority.
- Fine levels for Art. 5 breaches (Art. 99) were not re-read this session.
