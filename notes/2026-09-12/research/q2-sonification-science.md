## Findings

### 1. Field-defining documents

**Kramer et al. Sonification Report (NSF / ICAD).** The report exists under the title *Sonification Report: Status of the Field and Research Agenda*, "prepared for the National Science Foundation by members of the International Community for Auditory Display" [Kramer1999]. It is hosted on Bruce Walker's Georgia Tech Sonification Lab site and was announced to the AUDITORY list in 1999; secondary records also date it 1997 (the workshop) / 1999 (publication) — cite as 1999 unless the treatise needs the workshop date. I did not fetch the PDF, so I cannot quote its definition of sonification or its research-agenda headings; the widely repeated definition attributed to it ("the use of nonspeech audio to convey information") should be checked against the PDF before quoting.

**The Sonification Handbook (Hermann, Hunt & Neuhoff, eds., 2011).** Published by Logos Publishing House, Berlin, 2011; the full text is open access chapter-by-chapter at sonification.de/handbook [Hermann2011]. Chapters confirmed in this session:
- Ch. 2, Walker & Nees, *Theory of Sonification* [WalkerNees2011] (existence confirmed; content not fetched).
- Ch. 15, Grond & Berger, *Parameter Mapping Sonification*, pp. 363–397 [GrondBerger2011].
- Ch. 18, Vickers, *Sonification for Process Monitoring* [Vickers2011] — fetched; see §5.
The Handbook's standard taxonomy of techniques — audification (direct playback of data as a waveform), parameter-mapping sonification (data dimensions mapped to acoustic parameters such as pitch, loudness, tempo, timbre), model-based sonification (data configures a virtual acoustic system the user excites), and earcons / auditory icons (symbolic, discrete messages) — is the organisation of the Handbook's technique chapters; I confirmed only the parameter-mapping and process-monitoring chapters directly, so the chapter numbers for audification, model-based sonification and earcons/auditory icons are UNVERIFIED here (they are all on the sonification.de downloads page, which is the place to confirm them).

**Hermann's 2008 definition.** Hermann (ICAD 2008, *Taxonomy and definition for sonification and auditory display*) proposed that a sound is a sonification if it (a) reflects objective properties or relations in the input data, (b) is produced by a systematic transformation, (c) is reproducible (same data → same sound), and (d) the same technique can be applied to different data and repeatedly to the same data [Hermann2008 — UNVERIFIED; paraphrase from memory, do not quote].

### 2. Psychoacoustic mapping and polarity (Walker / Nees)

Walker's programme at Georgia Tech used magnitude-estimation methods to determine, for a given data dimension (temperature, pressure, velocity, size, dollars, etc.), which acoustic dimension listeners prefer and — critically — which **polarity** (does rising pitch mean "more" or "less"?) and what **scaling** (slope of the psychophysical function) they assume. A Georgia Tech repository record confirms work titled *Evaluating the magnitude estimation approach for designing sonification mapping topologies* [GT-magest — existence VERIFIED, authors/year UNVERIFIED]. The canonical journal statements are Walker (2002, JEP:Applied) and Walker (2007, Applied Psychoacoustics/Perception & Psychophysics) [UNVERIFIED]. The practical conclusions that survive in every later review (and in Walker & Nees's Handbook chapter): (i) polarity is not universal — it depends on the conceptual dimension and on the listener population (sighted vs. visually impaired users differ); (ii) pitch is the most discriminable and most used mapping dimension but has a limited number of absolutely identifiable levels; (iii) mappings must be validated with the target population rather than assumed. Numbers (e.g., percentage of participants preferring a given polarity) were not seen in this session and must not be stated.

### 3. The pulse-oximeter variable-pitch tone and the anaesthesia evidence base

**Origin.** The pitch-varying pulse tone (pitch falls as SpO2 falls) is generally attributed to Nellcor's first commercial oximeter (N-100, early 1980s) [UNVERIFIED — I did not find a primary source this session; the *Sounds of Desaturation* survey below likely gives the history and should be consulted]. It is the only widely deployed continuous sonification of a physiological variable and is the reference case for the whole field.

**Loeb & Fitch (2002), Anesthesia & Analgesia 94(2):362–368, DOI 10.1097/00000539-200202000-00025 [VERIFIED, abstract fetched from PubMed].** Design: an auditory display conveying **six** physiological variables in **two streams** — a cardiovascular sound triggered by each heartbeat (heart rate, SpO2, blood pressure) and a respiratory sound triggered by each breath (respiratory rate, end-tidal CO2, tidal volume). Fourteen anaesthesia residents monitored auditory, visual, and combined displays over 21 trials to detect and identify six predefined critical events. Results as reported: every subject detected every event in every format; detection was fastest with the combined display (**10.4 s**) vs. visual (**12.8 s**) and auditory-only (**13.0 s**); event *identification* accuracy was lowest with auditory-only (**60%**) vs. visual (**88%**) and combined (**80%**). Authors' conclusion (from the abstract): with little training, clinicians can detect and identify simulated events from an auditory display of six variables. Take-away for the treatise: continuous sonification alone was as good as visual for *detection* but worse for *diagnosis*; the combination was best — this is the pattern that recurs across the literature.

**Watson & Sanderson (2004), Human Factors 46(3):497–517, DOI 10.1518/hfes.46.3.497.50401, PMID 15573548 [VERIFIED bibliographic record via SAGE/PubMed; only a fragment of the abstract was returned].** Title: *Sonification Supports Eyes-Free Respiratory Monitoring and Task Time-Sharing*. Three experiments on auditory display of respiratory variables during anaesthesia; the abstract's conclusion is that sonification of patient physiology beyond traditional pulse oximetry "appears to be a viable and useful adjunct to visual monitors". The paper's core claim (title) is that a continuous respiratory sonification let participants monitor without looking while performing concurrent tasks — the key "eyes-free / time-sharing" evidence. Effect sizes were not visible in the fetched fragment; do not quote numbers.

**Sanderson group (Queensland) — "enhanced" oximetry sonifications.** A line of laboratory and simulation studies asks whether adding a second acoustic dimension to the pitch tone lets listeners identify SpO2 *ranges* (not just trends):
- Paterson et al. (2016), *Anaesthesia*, DOI 10.1111/anae.13424: sonification enhanced with tremolo and brightness for distinguishing clinically important SpO2 ranges, laboratory study [VERIFIED title/DOI via Wiley record; results not fetched].
- Schwarz & Sanderson (2017), *British Journal of Anaesthesia* 119(6):1224–, PMID 29045578: laboratory comparison of five auditory displays for conveying SpO2 ranges [VERIFIED title/venue/volume/pages via BJA/PubMed records; author list inferred from the request and the Arizona experts page — check].
- *Evaluation of an enhanced pulse oximeter auditory display: a simulation study* (PMID 32682554) [VERIFIED existence; venue/year/authors not confirmed].
- *Signaling Patient Oxygen Desaturation with Enhanced Pulse Oximetry Tones* (PMC9767428) [existence VERIFIED; fetch blocked].
- *Novel Pulse Oximetry Sonifications for Neonatal Oxygen Saturation Monitoring: A Laboratory Study* (Human Factors) and *The Sounds of Desaturation: A Survey of Commercial Pulse Oximeter Sonifications* [existence VERIFIED via ResearchGate records; authors/years UNVERIFIED]. The survey documents that commercial oximeters differ in how (and how much) pitch varies with SpO2 — i.e., the de-facto standard is not standardised.
The consistent qualitative finding across this line (from abstracts I have read previously, not fetched today — UNVERIFIED as to numbers) is that the plain pitch tone supports *trend* detection but not accurate *absolute* range identification, and that adding tremolo/brightness at threshold crossings improves range identification substantially without harming concurrent-task performance.

**Sanderson's broader "continuous informing" argument.** Sanderson and colleagues framed continuous auditory displays as *peripheral* displays: they keep the operator informed at low attentional cost and support "eyes-free" monitoring while the eyes and hands are engaged elsewhere (Sanderson, Watson & Russell 2005, *Anesth Analg*, "Advanced patient monitoring displays: tools for continuous informing"; Sanderson 2006, *Applied Ergonomics*, "The multimodal world of medical monitoring displays") [both UNVERIFIED this session]. The same group has also published on the *costs* of continuous sound (annoyance, interference with speech in the OR, and the need for volume/attenuation) — cited in later reviews but not fetched.

### 4. Alarm fatigue, masking, and the standards response

- **Alarm fatigue** (high false-positive rates → desensitisation, silencing, missed true alarms) is documented mostly in the clinical literature (ICU/telemetry). The canonical review is Sendelbach & Funk (2013), *AACN Advanced Critical Care* [UNVERIFIED]; ECRI has listed alarm hazards among its top technology hazards for years [UNVERIFIED]. The concept is directly transferable to control rooms (see ISA-18.2 / EEMUA 191 alarm-management literature in the process industries — outside this question's scope but the same phenomenon).
- **Masking and identification failures.** Momtahan, Hétu & Tansley (1993, *Ergonomics*) found OR and ICU staff could identify only a minority of the alarms in their own units and that many alarms were masked by others or by ambient noise [UNVERIFIED — the finding is widely cited; the numbers must be checked]. Judy Edworthy's work (Edworthy & Hellier; Edworthy et al. on IEC 60601-1-8 melodies) showed the 2006-standard melodic alarms were hard to learn and easily confused [UNVERIFIED].
- **IEC 60601-1-8** (medical electrical equipment — general requirements, tests and guidance for alarm systems). The 2006 edition specified a set of reserved melodic (multi-tone) alarm signals by priority and by category. The 2020 amendment (Amd 2) added a new, optionally usable set of "auditory-icon"-style alarm sounds developed from Edworthy's research, in response to evidence the melodies were poorly learned; AAMI reported this as "break[ing] new ground in development of alarm sounds" [AAMI article existence VERIFIED (title in search); fetch returned 403; the amendment details are from background knowledge — UNVERIFIED]. A 2019 Springer chapter, *A Review of Design Guidelines for Clinical Auditory Alarms* (DOI 10.1007/978-3-030-24067-7_37), consolidates the guideline literature [existence VERIFIED].
- **ISO 7731:2003** *Ergonomics — Danger signals for public and work areas — Auditory danger signals* [VERIFIED title via iso.org]. Specifies audibility/signal-to-noise requirements for danger signals in workplaces (level above masked threshold, spectral placement relative to background noise, temporal pattern) — the relevant standard for a control room where an alarm must be heard above ambient noise. Specific numeric requirements (e.g., dB above masked threshold) were not fetched — UNVERIFIED.
- **ISO 24500:2010** *Ergonomics — Accessible design — Auditory signals for consumer products* [VERIFIED title via iso.org]; ISO 24501 (sound pressure levels of auditory signals) and ISO 24502 (age-related luminance/related) are the neighbouring accessible-design standards [UNVERIFIED]. These are consumer-product oriented and only loosely applicable to control rooms.
- No ISO/IEC standard covers *continuous* (non-alarm) sonification of process state; the standards landscape is entirely about discrete alarms. This is itself a citable gap.

### 5. Sonification in process control, network, and SOC monitoring

- **Vickers, "Sonification for Process Monitoring", Sonification Handbook ch. 18 [VERIFIED, fetched].** Surveys auditory displays for monitoring live data and recommends that future work draw on semiotics/aesthetics, *soundscapes*, steganographic embedding (hiding data in sound the user already listens to), model-based sonification and spatialisation. Systems catalogued include Peep (network), Sonnet / Listen / CAITLIN / program-slice sonification (software/debugging), Hermann et al.'s auditory weather reports, and WebMelody (Barra et al. 2002 — server workload blended into user-chosen music). The chapter provides no quantitative evaluation summary of concurrent-stream limits or annoyance thresholds (checked in the fetched text).
- **Peep — Gilfix & Couch (2000), USENIX LISA XIV [VERIFIED, fetched].** A "sonic ecology" of natural sounds: discrete *events* → single sounds (bird chirps, woodpecker pecks); *states* → continuous backgrounds (waterfall, wind) whose intensity tracks load; *heartbeats* → periodic sounds (cricket chirps) whose rate signals liveness. Rationale, quoted: natural sounds "sound normal and pleasing in almost any combination similar to that of nature"; the design exploits "our ability to notice a deviation from the norm with little effort" and lets the administrator "hear peripherally whether action is required"; "If your email server dies … the crickets will have stopped chirping." **No formal quantitative evaluation is reported** — it is a design paper with anecdotal claims.
- **SOC sonification.** *Sonification in security operations centres: what do security practitioners think?* (Axon and colleagues, Oxford, 2018 — author list UNVERIFIED; existence VERIFIED) is an interview/survey study of practitioner attitudes rather than a performance study; the same group published on network-anomaly sonification design. *Network Situational Awareness: Sonification & Visualization in the Cyber Battlespace* [existence VERIFIED, authors UNVERIFIED] is a position/design paper. I found no controlled study demonstrating that continuous sonification improves SOC or NOC detection performance.
- **Industrial process control rooms (SCADA/DCS).** I found no controlled field or simulator study of *continuous* sonification of plant state in a process control room in this session. The relevant literature is instead (a) the anaesthesia work above, whose task structure (multivariable continuous monitoring, eyes elsewhere, high alarm load) is the closest analogue, and (b) alarm-management standards. This is the central gap for the treatise.

### 6. Multi-stream (polyphonic) monitoring limits and auditory scene analysis

- **Bregman (1990), *Auditory Scene Analysis*, MIT Press [UNVERIFIED].** Provides the perceptual theory: the auditory system groups acoustic components into "streams" by proximity in frequency, timbre, spatial location, onset synchrony and temporal regularity. Two sonifications will be heard as separate streams (and thus separately monitorable) only if they are segregated on these dimensions; streams that share register/timbre fuse or mask each other. This is the design basis for Peep's timbrally distinct natural sounds and for Loeb & Fitch's heartbeat vs. breath streams.
- **Empirical capacity.** The evidence in this session supports the following, and no more: Loeb & Fitch had residents track **six variables in two rhythmic streams** with 100% event detection but only 60% identification accuracy from audio alone [VERIFIED]; Watson & Sanderson showed a respiratory sonification could be monitored **concurrently with the pulse-oximeter tone** (two continuous streams) during other tasks [VERIFIED title-level]. Fitch & Kramer (1994, "Sonifying the body electric", in Kramer ed. *Auditory Display*, Addison-Wesley) is the earlier eight-variable demonstration often cited for the same design [UNVERIFIED]. Claims that operators can track "up to N" concurrent streams are not established; the literature suggests a practical ceiling of roughly two to three deliberately segregated streams for *monitoring* (as opposed to detecting the disappearance of a background), but I saw no paper stating a number, so the treatise should not state one either. Auditory-attention research (divided attention across streams degrades detection; change deafness) points the same way [UNVERIFIED].

## What an engineering treatise can legitimately say

- Sonification is an established research field with a canonical definition (Hermann 2008), an NSF-commissioned status report (Kramer et al. 1999), a peer-reviewed open-access handbook (Hermann, Hunt & Neuhoff 2011) and a stable technique taxonomy (audification; parameter-mapping; model-based; earcons/auditory icons). One can cite these for "what sonification is" without hedging.
- The best-evidenced claim for *continuous* sonification is from anaesthesia: a continuous auditory display supports eyes-free monitoring and task time-sharing (Watson & Sanderson 2004) and allows clinicians to *detect* events from sound alone at rates comparable to visual displays after little training (Loeb & Fitch 2002: 100% detection across formats; combined display fastest at 10.4 s vs 12.8 s visual vs 13.0 s audio). The same study shows audio alone is worse for *identifying* what happened (60% vs 88% visual). So: sound for detection and orientation, vision for diagnosis.
- The pulse-oximeter pitch tone is the single field-proven example of continuous sonification, and even it conveys trend well but absolute level poorly; the Queensland group's enhanced sonifications (tremolo/brightness at thresholds) are laboratory evidence that adding a second acoustic dimension improves range identification (Paterson 2016; Schwarz & Sanderson 2017) — cite as laboratory, not field, evidence.
- Mapping choices (which parameter, which polarity, what scaling) are empirical questions, not conventions; Walker's magnitude-estimation results show polarity depends on the data concept and the user population and must be validated with the intended operators.
- Ambient/soundscape monitoring of technical systems (Peep, WebMelody, SOC soundscapes) has a plausible perceptual rationale — humans detect deviations from a familiar background at low cost — and Vickers' Handbook chapter endorses the soundscape direction, but the published systems are design demonstrations without controlled performance data. A treatise may present the approach as promising and unproven, not as validated.
- Alarm fatigue and masking are well documented in clinical settings and drove a 2020 revision of IEC 60601-1-8; any sonification design must budget for annoyance, masking of speech and alarms (ISO 7731 sets audibility-over-noise requirements for danger signals), and operator ability to attenuate or silence the display.
- Concurrent-stream capacity is bounded by auditory scene analysis (Bregman): streams must be segregated by timbre, register, rhythm or location. Demonstrated designs use two rhythmic streams carrying up to six variables; no paper establishes a larger number, so the treatise should state a design rule ("few, well-segregated streams; background sounds whose *absence* is salient") rather than a capacity figure.
- Known limit: there is no controlled evidence, and no standard, for continuous sonification of plant or team state in an industrial control room. Every performance claim transfers from anaesthesia or laboratory studies and should be labelled as such.

## Gaps

- No fetched text for the Kramer report, Walker & Nees chapter, Hermann 2008, or Bregman — quotations and chapter/page numbers for these must be pulled before print.
- Watson & Sanderson 2004 effect sizes (task-sharing performance, detection rates by condition) were not obtained; the SAGE/PubMed abstract should be read in full.
- The Queensland enhanced-oximetry results (percent-correct range identification per display) were not obtained; Paterson 2016 and Schwarz & Sanderson 2017 abstracts contain them.
- Origin of the variable-pitch oximeter tone (Nellcor N-100, ~1983) is unconfirmed; the *Sounds of Desaturation* survey is the likely primary source.
- IEC 60601-1-8 Amd 2 (2020) details, ISO 7731 numeric audibility requirements, and Momtahan et al. 1993 identification percentages are all unverified.
- No industrial control room / SCADA / DCS study of continuous sonification was found; a targeted search of ICAD proceedings (icad.org), *Human Factors*, *Ergonomics*, and *Applied Ergonomics* for "process control" + "auditory display" is the next step, as is the aviation literature on continuous auditory displays (e.g., engine-sound cues) which was not searched.
- Team-state (as opposed to plant-state) sonification appears entirely unexplored in the sources seen; the closest analogue is SOC-practitioner attitude work.
