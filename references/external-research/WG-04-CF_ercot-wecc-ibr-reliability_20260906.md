# External research: ERCOT, WECC and NERC IBR reliability sources

External research; found via valyu, not the working group's own analysis.

Supports the North American facts a future paper, *ERCOT and WECC Renewable
Integration Challenges: Inverter-Based Resource Reliability Under Stress
Conditions* (J. McKenney byline, not yet written), will need to cite: the
"43% inverter-based resource capacity" and "peak renewable penetration above
75%" claim; the framing of ERCOT as a "preview" for other interconnections;
the "WECC interconnection queue times averaging 5 years, up from under 2
years in 2008" claim; the NERC Alert language on unexpected IBR tripping
during faults; and the ERCOT critical-inertia figure (100 vs. 105 GW.s).

> **WARNING 1: the "43% inverter-based resource capacity" figure could not
> be matched to any ERCOT primary source and should not be published as
> written.** No ERCOT Fact Sheet, CDR, or SARA report retrieved for this
> file states an IBR share of installed capacity of 43% for any date. The
> closest bracketing data points, both from ERCOT's own Fact Sheet: as of
> November 2021, wind (24.8%) + solar (3.8%) + storage (0.2%) = 28.8% of
> installed generating capacity; as of August 2026, wind (22.4%) + solar
> (19.7%) + storage (2.9%) = 45.0%. If the drafter needs a dated
> capacity-mix percentage near 43%, it has to be pulled from an ERCOT Fact
> Sheet or CDR dated to the specific year being discussed and quoted
> directly; do not carry the 43% figure forward on the strength of this
> file.

> **WARNING 2: "peak renewable penetration above 75%" is close to a real,
> dated ERCOT record but the two numbers in the claim are not from the same
> moment and should not be presented as simultaneous.** ERCOT's own Monthly
> report for April 2024 states a renewable penetration record of 75.67% was
> set March 29, 2024, at 2:13 p.m., with renewable generation of 34,958 MW
> at that instant (Source 5). This is a single-instant record, not a
> sustained operating condition, and it postdates the November 2021 Fact
> Sheet capacity mix by more than two years. Do not pair "43% capacity" and
> "75% penetration" as if ERCOT was simultaneously in both states; state the
> date of each figure separately, per the hard rule.

> **WARNING 3: no credible body was found calling ERCOT a "leading
> indicator" or "preview" for other interconnections in the specific
> resource-adequacy-and-weather-stress sense the quoted sentence uses.** See
> Source 6 for what NERC's own Odessa disturbance reports actually say
> about ERCOT's relevance to other regions, which is narrower: solar PV
> performance issues (not resource adequacy or weather stress broadly) are
> called a risk "in many areas of North America," not that ERCOT is a
> leading indicator of that risk. The sentence in the citing paper should be
> treated as the working group's own synthesis unless a better source is
> found, per the vault's sourcing rule.

> **WARNING 4: "WECC interconnection queue times averaging 5 years, up from
> under 2 years in 2008" is a national U.S. figure in LBNL's Queued Up
> series, not a WECC-specific one, and must be corrected before
> publication.** LBNL's report aggregates all seven ISO/RTO queues plus
> dozens of non-ISO utilities, including the non-ISO West and CAISO, which
> together are part of but not synonymous with WECC. LBNL does not publish
> a single "WECC" queue-duration figure in the editions retrieved for this
> file. The "5 years... under 2 years in 2008" language is real and stable
> across multiple editions (see Source 3) but describes the whole U.S., not
> WECC. Correct the claim to "U.S. interconnection queue times" or similar,
> or find a WECC-specific figure; do not keep "WECC" as the scope.

> **WARNING 5: ERCOT's own primary source gives 100 GW.s as Critical
> Inertia and resolves the 100-vs-105 conflict, but 105 GW.s is a real
> ERCOT figure too, for a different purpose.** ERCOT's 2018 public
> whitepaper (Source 7) states plainly: "Based on this analysis, and with a
> safety margin, ERCOT has identified its Critical Inertia to be 100 GW*s."
> That is the System Operating Limit. Separately, the same document states
> that "When system inertia drops below 105,000 MW*s, the ERCOT Control
> Room will take actions to restore system inertia to levels at or above
> 105,000 MW*s" -- an operational restoration trigger ERCOT set with margin
> above the 100 GW.s limit, not the limit itself. Any secondary source
> reporting "105 GW.s" as ERCOT's critical inertia limit has conflated the
> restoration trigger with the System Operating Limit. Use 100 GW.s for
> "Critical Inertia" and, if the 105 GW.s figure is used at all, label it
> the control-room restoration trigger, not a limit.

## Source 1: NERC/Texas RE, Odessa Disturbance Report (2021 event)

- Title: Odessa Disturbance, Texas Events: May 9, 2021 and June 26, 2021,
  Joint NERC and Texas RE Staff Report
- URL: https://www.nerc.com/globalassets/our-work/reports/event-reports/odessa_disturbance_report.pdf
- Retrieved: 2026-09-06 (full PDF text extracted)
- Publication date: September 2021
- Query run: "NERC inverter-based resource performance disturbance report
  Odessa"
- Supports: claim 4 (unexpected IBR tripping during faults, documented by
  NERC)
- Summary: at 11:21 a.m. Central on May 9, 2021, a single-line-to-ground
  fault occurred on a generator step-up transformer at a combined-cycle
  plant near Odessa, Texas, "caused by a failed surge arrester at the
  combustion turbine (CT) during startup for testing." The fault cleared in
  three cycles. Table ES.1 gives the exact reduction by unit type:
  Combined Cycle Plant 192 MW, Solar PV Plants 1,112 MW, Wind Plants 36 MW,
  Total 1,340 MW. The report states plainly: "None of the affected
  inverter-based resources were tripped consequentially by the fault
  itself. Rather, all reductions were due to inverter-level or feeder-level
  tripping or control system behavior within the resources." Predisturbance
  mix: wind 34%, solar PV 9%, synchronous generation 56% of ERCOT
  generation. A second, smaller event on June 26, 2021, is analyzed in
  Appendix C of the same report (not extracted in detail for this file).

## Source 2: NERC/Texas RE, 2022 Odessa Disturbance Report (June 4, 2022 event)

- Title: 2022 Odessa Disturbance, Texas Event: June 4, 2022, Joint NERC and
  Texas RE Staff Report
- URL: https://www.nerc.com/comm/RSTC_Reliability_Guidelines/NERC_2022_Odessa_Disturbance_Report%20(1).pdf
- Retrieved: 2026-09-06 (full PDF text extracted)
- Publication date: December 2022
- Query run: "NERC inverter-based resource performance disturbance report
  Odessa"
- Supports: claim 4
- Summary: at 12:59:25 p.m. Central on June 4, 2022, "a surge arrestor
  failed at a synchronous generation facility in Odessa, Texas, causing a
  B-phase-to-ground fault on the 345 kV system. The fault cleared in three
  cycles, disconnecting part of the plant that was carrying 333 MW. Other
  units in the plant unexpectedly tripped for an additional immediate loss
  of 202 MW. A separate synchronous generation facility in South Texas over
  450 miles away lost an additional 309 MW. In total, 844 MW of synchronous
  generation tripped at the time of the disturbance. In addition, 1,711 MW
  of inverter-based resources from many different facilities also
  unexpectedly reduced power output due to the protection and controls at
  each site. Therefore, the normally-cleared single-line-to-ground fault
  resulted in a total loss of 2,555 MW of generation, and system frequency
  dropped to 59.7 Hz." Table ES.1 confirms: Synchronous Generation Plants
  844 MW, Solar PV Plants 1,711 MW, Total 2,555 MW. Predisturbance mix:
  synchronous 73.5%, wind 10.4%, solar PV 15.8%. The report calls this "a
  perfect illustration of the need for immediate industry action" and notes
  the combined loss "nearly exceeded the Texas Interconnection Resource
  Loss Protection Criteria" defined in NERC BAL-003.

## Source 3: LBNL, Queued Up: 2024 Edition

- Title: Queued Up: 2024 Edition, Characteristics of Power Plants Seeking
  Transmission Interconnection As of the End of 2023
- URL: https://emp.lbl.gov/sites/default/files/2024-04/Queued%20Up%202024%20Edition_1.pdf
- Retrieved: 2026-09-06 (full PDF text extracted)
- Publication date: April 2024 (data as of end of 2023)
- Query run: "Lawrence Berkeley Queued Up interconnection queue duration
  report"
- Supports: claim 3, with the correction in WARNING 4
- Summary: "The typical project built in 2023 took nearly 5 years from the
  interconnection request to commercial operations, compared to 3 years in
  2015 and <2 years in 2008." This is a national figure covering "all seven
  ISOs/RTOs in concert with... non-ISO utilities, representing an estimated
  85% of all U.S. electricity load" (methodology description repeated
  across editions). The same wording, with the year updated, appears in the
  2022-data edition (published April 2023): "The typical project built in
  2022 took 5 years from the interconnection request to commercial
  operations1, compared to 3 years in 2015 and <2 years in 2008." The 2025
  edition (data as of end of 2024, published December 2025) reports a
  further increase to 55 months (4.5 years) for projects reaching
  commercial operation in 2024, per a secondary summary of that edition
  (Source 4); the underlying LBNL PDF for the 2025 edition was not opened
  in full for this file. None of the editions retrieved break out a
  region-specific "WECC" queue duration; regional breakdowns given are by
  ISO/RTO (CAISO, ERCOT, MISO, NYISO, PJM, SPP, ISO-NE) plus "non-ISO West,"
  which is part of WECC's footprint but not the whole of it and is reported
  only for queue volume (e.g., "706 GW" in the West per an April 2025 LBNL
  slide deck), not queue duration.

## Source 6: NERC, 2022 Odessa Disturbance Report (leading-indicator language check)

- Title: 2022 Odessa Disturbance report (same document as Source 2)
- URL: https://www.nerc.com/comm/RSTC_Reliability_Guidelines/NERC_2022_Odessa_Disturbance_Report%20(1).pdf
- Retrieved: 2026-09-06
- Query run: "NERC ERCOT leading indicator other interconnections renewable
  integration lessons"
- Supports: claim 2, and closes it as unsupported in the specific form
  quoted; see WARNING 3
- Summary: the report's Executive Summary states, quoted exactly: "As the
  penetration of solar PV resources (and all inverter-based resources)
  continues to grow rapidly in the ERCOT footprint and in many areas of
  North America, it is paramount that these inverter-based resource
  performance issues are proactively and immediately addressed." This is
  NERC treating ERCOT's IBR performance issues as a shared, growing risk
  across North America, not a statement that ERCOT specifically previews
  outcomes for other interconnections, and it is scoped to inverter
  ride-through/tripping performance, not to the broader resource-adequacy
  and weather-stress framing in the quoted sentence under review. A
  separate web search for a body-level statement calling ERCOT a "leading
  indicator" specifically for resource adequacy and weather-extreme
  operational stress returned only trade-press commentary on ERCOT's
  "connect and manage" interconnection process being watched by SPP/MISO
  (Utility Dive, June 2025 and November 2023) -- a different topic
  (interconnection speed, not IBR reliability under stress) and not a
  citable authority for the sentence as currently worded.

## Source 7: ERCOT, Inertia: Basic Concepts and Impacts on the ERCOT Grid

- Title: Inertia: Basic Concepts and Impacts on the ERCOT Grid (ERCOT Public
  Paper)
- URL: https://www.ercot.com/files/docs/2018/04/04/Inertia_Basic_Concepts_Impacts_On_ERCOT_v0.pdf
- Retrieved: 2026-09-06 (full PDF text extracted)
- Publication date: April 4, 2018 (version 0, the only version located)
- Query run: "ERCOT critical inertia limit GW.s frequency"
- Supports: claim 5, and resolves the 100-vs-105 conflict per WARNING 5
- Summary: this is ERCOT's own methodology document defining Critical
  Inertia as "the minimum inertia level at which a system can be reliably
  operated with current frequency control practices." For a generation loss
  of 2,750 MW (the two largest units in the system), the paper derives that
  frequency must take at least 0.416 seconds to fall from 59.7 Hz to 59.3
  Hz (the first-stage Under-Frequency Load Shed trigger) for Load Resources
  to respond in time. Using a regression across 13 dynamic-simulation
  cases, "Critical Inertia was found to be 94 GW*s when Tf is 0.416 sec.
  Based on this analysis, and with a safety margin, ERCOT has identified
  its Critical Inertia to be 100 GW*s." The paper separately describes a
  four-level control-room alarming scheme: green at or above 120,000 MW*s,
  yellow 110,000-119,999 MW*s, orange 100,001-109,999 MW*s, red at or below
  100,000 MW*s, and states operationally: "When system inertia drops below
  105,000 MW*s, the ERCOT Control Room will take actions to restore system
  inertia to levels at or above 105,000 MW*s." The lowest inertia ERCOT had
  experienced as of this paper's writing was "130 GW*s on October 27,
  2017." This is a 2018 document; ERCOT has continued to track and publish
  inertia data since (per the ERCOT IBR integration reports page), so a
  more recent ERCOT-published Critical Inertia figure should be checked
  before publication in case the SOL has since been revised.

## Source 8: NERC, 900 MW Fault Induced Solar PV Resource Interruption Disturbance Report (Canyon 2 Fire)

- Title: 900 MW Fault Induced Solar Photovoltaic Resource Interruption
  Disturbance Report, Southern California Event: October 9, 2017, Joint
  NERC and WECC Staff Report
- URL: https://www.nerc.com/globalassets/our-work/reports/event-reports/900-mw-solar-photovoltaic-resource-interruption-disturbance-report.pdf
- Retrieved: 2026-09-06 (full PDF text extracted)
- Publication date: February 2018
- Query run: "NERC Blue Cut Fire disturbance report solar photovoltaic
  tripping"
- Supports: claim 4 (a second, earlier, WECC-territory example alongside
  Odessa)
- Summary: "On October 9, 2017, the Canyon 2 Fire caused two transmission
  system faults near the Serrano substation east of Los Angeles. The first
  fault was a normally cleared phase-to-phase fault on a 220 kV
  transmission line that occurred at 12:12:16 Pacific time, and the second
  fault was a normally cleared phase-to-phase fault on a 500 kV
  transmission line that occurred at 12:14:30 Pacific time." Table 1.1
  gives exact figures: Event 1 (220 kV, line-to-line AB, 2.85 cycles to
  clear) lost 682 MW; Event 2 (500 kV, line-to-line AC, 2.86 cycles to
  clear) lost 937 MW; the report's title characterizes the combined event
  as "Approximately 900 MW of solar PV resources were lost." Cause, quoted
  exactly: "the majority of inverter tripping was caused by sub-cycle
  transient overvoltages and instantaneous protective action at the
  inverters to disconnect them from the grid. A significant amount of
  inverters also entered momentary cessation during and following the
  fault events." The 500 kV fault caused a Western Interconnection
  frequency nadir of 59.878 Hz about 3.3 seconds after the fault, recovering
  to nominal in roughly 100 seconds. The report explicitly credits the
  earlier Blue Cut Fire disturbance report with eliminating erroneous
  frequency tripping by this event: "No frequency-related tripping occurred
  during either of the two Canyon 2 Fire fault events. It appears that the
  mitigating actions of the inverter manufacturer and affected GOs has
  remediated the frequency-related issues identified during the Blue Cut
  Fire disturbance." CAISO's own solar PV penetration is also given: 47.3%
  peak (May 4, 2017) and 34.3% at the time of this disturbance (October 9,
  2017), both including distribution-connected solar.

## Source 9: Blue Cut Fire disturbance (secondary sources; primary PDF not retrievable)

- Titles: "NERC Recommends Inverter Changes After California Fire Disrupts
  Solar Generation" (Troutman Sanders Washington Energy Report) and
  "Assuring Reliability in an Evolving Reliability Landscape" (T&D World)
- URLs:
  https://www.troutmanenergyreport.com/2017/06/nerc-recommends-inverter-changes-california-fire-disrupts-solar-generation/
  and
  https://www.tdworld.com/transmission-reliability/article/20971327/assuring-reliability-in-an-evolving-reliability-landscape
- Retrieved: 2026-09-06
- Query run: "NERC Blue Cut Fire disturbance report solar photovoltaic
  tripping"
- Supports: claim 4 (the earliest of the four named events)
- Note on why this is secondary, not primary: the NERC PDF at
  http://www.nerc.com/pa/rrm/ea/1200_MW_Fault_Induced_Solar_Photovoltaic_Resource_/1200_MW_Fault_Induced_Solar_Photovoltaic_Resource_Interruption_Final.pdf
  (and the NERC report-listing page that links it) both failed to extract
  for this file across two attempts; the file may be behind an access
  control or simply not machine-readable via this tool. The event and its
  headline MW figure are independently corroborated by NERC's own later
  Canyon 2 Fire report (Source 8), which references "the Blue Cut Fire
  disturbance report" by name and describes it as the source of the
  frequency-tripping and 5-second momentary-cessation-recovery
  recommendations that Canyon 2 confirms were subsequently implemented.
- Summary: on August 16, 2016, the Blue Cut Fire in Southern California's
  Cajon Pass caused faults on transmission lines in a corridor carrying
  solar PV output; both sources give "the loss of 1,200 megawatts (MW) of
  solar photovoltaic (PV) power generation." Cause, per Troutman: "the
  solar PV resources disconnected as a result of an inaccurate perception
  of the system's frequency. The system's inverters, which were set to
  trip instantaneously in response to a simultaneous reading of a lower or
  higher than normal frequency, erroneously tripped upon perceiving the
  faults on the system," and separately, inverters using momentary
  cessation "returned to pre-disturbance operation levels too slowly,"
  leading to the recommendation that "such inverters should be set to
  delay the restoration of output by no more than five seconds." T&D World
  independently confirms the same MW figure and cause category: "This
  report identified that a large amount of solar photovoltaic resources
  erroneously tripped on instantaneously calculated frequency measurements.
  In addition, many inverters also used an operating mode called
  'momentary cessation'... when terminal voltage falls outside an
  acceptable operating range." Because this file could not open the NERC
  PDF directly, treat the exact MW figure (1,200 MW) as corroborated by two
  independent secondary sources plus a NERC cross-reference, but not as a
  primary-source-verified quote in the way Sources 1, 2, and 8 are.

## Gaps: what could NOT be sourced

1. **The "43% inverter-based resource capacity" figure has no matching
   ERCOT primary source at any date checked (2021, 2023, 2024, 2026).** See
   WARNING 1. This should be treated as unsupported until a dated ERCOT
   Fact Sheet, CDR, or SARA snapshot showing exactly that percentage is
   found, or dropped from the paper.
2. **No credible body (NERC, ERCOT, FERC, WECC, DOE) was found describing
   ERCOT as a "leading indicator" or "preview" specifically for resource
   adequacy and weather-extreme operational stress in other
   interconnections.** See WARNING 3 and Source 6. NERC's language on IBR
   performance risk spreading "in many areas of North America" is the
   closest available and is narrower in scope than the sentence under
   review.
3. **No WECC-specific interconnection queue duration figure was found in
   LBNL's Queued Up series.** The "5 years, up from under 2 years in 2008"
   language is real but national, not WECC-specific; see WARNING 4 and
   Source 3. LBNL gives West/non-ISO queue volume, not duration, separately
   from the national duration trend.
4. **The Blue Cut Fire NERC PDF itself could not be opened by this tool**
   (two direct URLs, two failures); its content is corroborated only
   through secondary sources and a cross-reference in NERC's later Canyon 2
   Fire report. See Source 9.
5. **A NERC Alert specifically and solely about "unexpected tripping of
   inverter-based resources during faults" was located** (the March 14,
   2023 Level 2 Industry Recommendation,
   https://www.nerc.com/globalassets/programs/bpsa/alerts/2023/nerc-alert-r-2023-03-14-01-level-2---inverter-based-resource-performance-issues.pdf)
   **but was not separately opened in full for quotable text beyond the
   excerpt captured in the initial search result**, which already confirms
   its date, scope (BES solar PV, and encouraged for BESS), and the
   background events it cites (the 2021 and 2022 Odessa disturbances). If
   the paper wants a direct quote from the alert itself rather than from
   the disturbance reports it is based on, that PDF should be fetched in
   full separately.
6. **Whether ERCOT's 100 GW.s Critical Inertia System Operating Limit has
   been revised since the 2018 whitepaper was not checked.** ERCOT's
   inertia monitoring and forecasting work has continued (referenced in a
   2021 ESIG conference slide as still "100 GWs" as of that presentation,
   not separately verified as primary for this file); a current,
   dated ERCOT statement of Critical Inertia should be located before
   publication if precision matters to the paper's argument.
