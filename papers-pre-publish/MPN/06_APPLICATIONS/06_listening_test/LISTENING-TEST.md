# Which scale fits which kind of character?

## 1. The honest version of why you are being asked

A composer scoring a scene decides in a few seconds what a character sounds like. Ask why the cue is in one mode rather than another and the answer is usually a gesture: the scene is weightless, the character is not disillusioned yet, that raised fourth holds the moment open. The judgement is normally right and almost never reproducible.

This project is trying to find out whether any of that can be written down as a rule that a second person could check. Not to replace anybody's ear. To find out whether the thing the ear is doing has a stateable shape.

There is a specific question underneath. A theory in this project says that three recognisable kinds of dramatic predicament each belong with a particular scale, and that the choice is not taste but consequence. Four different versions of that claim exist in the project's own documents, and two of them are close to opposites. So somebody has to listen.

We have already run this test on a panel of language models. They agreed with each other strongly and they failed the one control we could check them against, so their answer is not worth much on its own. That is why we need people who actually score drama for a living.

## 2. What you are not being told, and why

You are not being told which scales these are, what the theory predicts, or what the models said. If you knew any of that you would not be able to unknow it, and the answer would be worth nothing.

You are also not being told which of the questions is a check. One of the situations described in Part 2 is there to find out whether people will confidently answer anything they are asked. Every question therefore has a "nothing here fits in any principled way" option, and choosing it is a real answer, not a failure to complete the test. If a question seems like nonsense, say so. That is the most useful thing you can do with it.

## 3. How to do it

**Listen before you look.** The audio comes first, in Parts 1, 2 and 3. The notation is in Part 4 and you should not open it until you get there. If you are the kind of musician who can hear a scale and name it in two bars, that is exactly why the order matters: we want the judgement your ear makes about the sound, then separately whatever your training tells you about the name.

Everything you hear is the same eight-bar melody, at the same tempo, in the same key, over the same low pedal, played on the same neutral tone. Only the scale changes. Nothing else in any file is different from anything else.

Work through it in one sitting if you can. Do not go back and revise Part 2 after you have done Part 3.

## 4. Part 1: six of them, ranked

Listen to these six from **Set 1**: A, B, D, E, F and C.

Put them in order from the one that sounds **happiest** to the one that sounds **saddest**. We are asking what the music sounds like it is expressing, not how it makes you feel, which is a different question and often gets a different answer.

Write the six letters in order, happiest first. Ties are allowed; say so if you have one.

## 5. Part 2: three situations, and one more

Here are four situations a character can be in. They are written as a director might describe them, not as a music cue sheet.

> **Situation 1.** Something has happened to this character that will not go away and cannot be explained. It is not a problem to be solved; it is a fact that does not yield. However they try to account for it, it comes back exactly as it was. This is the thing a character keeps returning to and can never get past.

> **Situation 2.** This character's world is held in place by role, rule, duty and position. Who they are is where they stand: king, daughter, soldier, debtor, servant. What is at stake in the scene is whether that order holds, and where they sit inside it.

> **Situation 3.** This character lives through a picture of themselves and measures themselves constantly against other people. Vanity, rivalry, admiration, and a version of themselves they can never quite become.

> **Situation 4.** This character's attention rises and falls on a fixed cycle that has nothing to do with what is happening around them. What matters is where in the cycle they are, so that events arrive early or late with respect to it.

For each of the four, listening to all seven of **Set 1** (A to G), answer:

- **Which one** would you use to score a character in that situation? One letter, or "none".
- **How confident**, from 0 to 100.
- **Why**, in one sentence. Say what it is about the sound, not what the scale is called.
- **At its worst.** If that situation is at its most extreme and most painful, does your answer change? To which letter, and why.

- **And if it changes, how does it change?** This is the question we most want a professional to settle, so it gets its own answer. When a situation goes to its worst, does the music want a *darker version of the same thing*, the same colour deepened, or does it want to *break to somewhere else entirely*, a different quality rather than a lower one? One of the theories under test says the first, that each kind of character has a pair of scales and extremity picks the darker of the pair. Something in our early results says the second, that extremity jumps to a different place altogether and the pair is the wrong idea. You are better placed to answer that than anyone, and either answer is useful. If it depends on which situation, say which and why.

And one question a composer can answer that nobody else can:

- **Would you set the cue in it, or pass through it?** For your chosen letter, would you write the cue in that scale as its home, or would you touch it and leave? This matters because the theory assumes the scale is where the cue lives, and if that is not how you work, the theory is asking the wrong question.

## 6. Part 3: the same four situations, a different family

**Set 2** is seven scales from a family that has no name, no history and no reputation. Nobody has written anything about what they mean, because until last week they did not exist as a set.

Do exactly the same thing: for each of the four situations, one letter from Set 2 or "none", a confidence, and a one-sentence reason.

This part is the one that matters most. If your Set 1 answers come from what the sound does, they should transfer here. If they come from what you were taught these scales mean, they will not, and we will be able to see the difference.

## 7. Part 4: now you may look

Open the notation, and answer honestly. There is no wrong answer here and nothing depends on you having been ignorant.

- Do you recognise Set 1 as a family you know? Name it if so, and name each of A to G.
- Did you know what they were while you were doing Parts 1 to 3? Throughout, partly, or not at all.
- If you knew: were your Part 2 answers coming from the sound, or from what these scales are conventionally said to express? Nobody will think less of you for the second. It is the thing we most need to know.

## 8. Two open questions, if you have the patience

- Is "which scale for which character" even the right question? If you would distinguish these three situations by some other means, register, texture, rhythm, silence, harmonic rhythm, say what you would actually do.
- Does the fourth situation strike you as a real thing a character can be in? Your answer to that is a control on everything else in this test.

## 9. What happens to your answers

They go into a research paper as data, with the design and the analysis published alongside, including whatever they turn out to say. If they contradict the theory, the paper says so; that is the point of asking. You can be named, named as a professional of a stated kind without your name, or anonymous. Say which.

If enough people do this, it becomes the first piece of evidence in the project that the system did not produce about itself.
