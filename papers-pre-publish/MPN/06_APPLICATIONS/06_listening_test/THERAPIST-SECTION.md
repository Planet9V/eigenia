# What a therapist can tell us that a composer cannot

## 1. Why you are being asked separately

Do the main test first. You listen and answer as everyone else does, and your answers go in with theirs. This part is a different thing and should be done afterwards, because some of it will tell you what the main test is careful not to.

The scales in that test come from a system built to score drama. One of that system's published descriptions told therapists they could track a client's psychological state over time and use the music as a discussion tool in a session. Nobody with clinical training was asked whether that was a good idea before it was written. That text is being withdrawn and replaced, and the replacement is in this pack so you can check that it says what we say it says. The questions below are what should have been asked first.

They are not a consultation on how to do it well. They include whether it should be done at all, and we would rather have that answer than a courteous one.

## 2. What the system is, and what is wrong with it

You should not be asked to judge something whose limits have been kept from you. All of the following is stated in the project's own theory paper and has been confirmed against the code.

The system takes a nine-part description of a character and turns it into a score. For a script imported through the product, four of those nine parts are not read from the text at all but assigned by calls to the random number generator, and on one path two more, the ones the project calls trauma and entropy, are assigned the same way. Three values a user sets in the interface are discarded before they reach the music. The generator is not seeded, so the same input does not produce the same output twice: press render again and you get a different piece.

No quantity in this theory has ever been measured by anything except the system itself. The large set of worked examples in the project is the system's own reading of the author's written commentary on some plays, so the numbers are not an independent assignment by any person. Two listener studies exist, and neither can be cited as evidence about anything, because the stimuli came from the unseeded generator and cannot be reproduced. One of the two is a null result.

The listening test exists because the project has never once had a number it did not produce about itself. It does not repair any of the above.

## 3. How to answer

Answer as the clinician you are, not as a reviewer being fair to a project. You are not being asked to accept that the three situations are real, that a state can be mapped to a scale, or that a generated cue has any business in a session. Every question below can be answered by someone who thinks the whole idea is misconceived, and several are asking for precisely that. Where a question rests on a premise you reject, name the premise rather than grading the question. Where you have no view, say so, since a therapist declining to answer is itself information we do not have.

## 4. The questions

**The three situations.** These are Situations 1, 2 and 3 from Part 2 of the main test. Situation 4 is discussed there and not here.

1. **Recognition.** Do the first three situations describe anyone you have worked with, or do they describe characters? Answer for each separately if they differ.

2. **Help or hazard.** If a clinician reads one of those descriptions and a particular client comes to mind, is that recognition useful, or is it the first step of a mistake? If it is a mistake, say what the mistake is.

3. **The one you would refuse.** Is there one of the three you would not use as a description of a living person under any circumstances? Which, and what is wrong with it.

4. **What you would have said instead.** If you had to describe what a person is going through to somebody who was going to make music out of it, would you say anything resembling these? If not, what would you actually say.

**The session.**

5. **The first five minutes.** A client hears a piece that was generated from a description of their state. Walk us through what happens next in the room, as concretely as you can.

6. **When it is wrong.** The client says the music is not right. The system says it is. Who decides, what do you do, and what has just happened to the session.

7. **When it is right, which may be the harder case.** The client says it is exactly right and is moved by it. Is that a good session or a problem, and what do you do with it.

8. **Authority.** Does music that arrives from a machine carry a different authority in the room than music you chose or played yourself? If so, in which direction, and does that difference help you or work against you.

**Harm.**

9. **Who should not be in the room with this.** Name the presentations you would rule out, and give the mechanism rather than the category: what goes wrong, to whom, how quickly, and whether you would see it happening.

10. **Harm to the work rather than to the client.** Is there a way this damages the relationship, your own attention, or the client's trust in you, without harming the client directly.

11. **The sound itself.** Before any question of meaning, what would have to be true of the audio, including level, onset, predictability and how a client stops it, before you would play a generated piece to anyone.

**Consent.**

12. **Consent when the output is a claim about the person.** What would a client have to be told, in what words, and at what point.

13. **Disclosing how the numbers arise.** Would you tell a client that some of the values behind their piece were assigned at random, and that the same description produces a different piece each time? If you would, is there anything left to consent to? If you would not, say why not.

14. **Where it goes afterwards.** The state values and the audio are a record of something. Who holds them, who may see them, and what would you refuse to let happen to them.

**Supervision and your own standing.**

15. **A reading of the client, in the room.** Is "the system's reading of this client" a thing you would ever want present in a session? If it is, under what supervision, how often, and who else has to be able to see it. If it is not, say so plainly, and say whether anything would change your mind.

16. **Your position.** Does using something like this touch your scope of practice, your registration, your insurance, or what your professional body would say to you. We would rather learn this from you now than discover it later.

**Before anyone tries it, and whether anyone should.**

17. **The thing you would need to see.** Name the specific demonstration or the specific result, not a level of evidence, that would have to exist before you would try this with one person.

18. **The smallest first use.** If there is a version of this small enough to be safe, describe it. If there is not, say there is not.

19. **Where it goes wrong.** If the idea is wrong, we want the first false step rather than a verdict. Point to the place: the description of the state, the assignment of a state to a person, the generation, the playing of it to the person, the interpretation afterwards, or something earlier than all of these.

20. **The other direction.** There is a version in which nobody is read by anything: the client moves the controls until the music matches how they feel, and that piece is what the session works with. Is that a different proposition or the same one with better manners? Answer without assuming we prefer either.

21. **What we did not ask.** What should have been on this list and is not.

## 5. What happens to your answers

They go into a research paper as data, with the design and the analysis published alongside, including whatever they turn out to say. If the therapists who answer say the clinical application should not exist, the paper says so and the application stops; that is a real outcome of asking and not a formality. You can be named, named as a professional of a stated kind without your name, or anonymous. Say which.
