# Music therapy and autism as an application domain: evidence, use cases and what is defensible

Written 2026-09-12 from four research passes filed at `ms-research/a1` to `a4`, each marking every citation verified or unverified. This is analysis, not a paper. Nothing here is a claim the system can make yet.

## 1. The short answer

There is a real application here, but it is not the obvious one. The obvious version, a system that reads a person's psychological state and plays it back to help them, is the version that is regulated as a medical device in both major jurisdictions, that no evidence supports, that the flagship trial in this field argues against, and that carries a specific risk of harm to the population it most wants to serve. The defensible version inverts the direction of the instrument: the person composes their own state rather than being told what it sounds like. That version makes no health claim, sits inside a credentialed therapist's practice, requires no regulatory pathway, and contributes something that only a generator can contribute.

## 2. Four findings that constrain everything

**The flagship trial was null, and the claim people repeat is not the claim that survived.** TIME-A randomised 364 autistic children aged four to seven across ten centres in nine countries to improvisational music therapy or enhanced standard care. The difference on the primary outcome, ADOS social affect, was 0.06, with a confidence interval from minus 0.70 to 0.81 and a p value of 0.88. Seventeen of twenty secondary outcomes were null. The authors wrote that the findings do not support improvisational music therapy for symptom reduction. Cochrane moved with it: the 2014 review of ten studies reported large effects on social interaction and communication, and the 2022 update of twenty-six studies and 1,165 participants has all three of those outcomes crossing the null at low to very low certainty. What survives at moderate certainty is a different set of claims: global improvement with a risk ratio of 1.22, quality of life at a standardised mean difference of 0.28, and total autism severity at minus 0.83. So "music therapy improves autistic social communication" is not supportable. "Music therapy is associated with global improvement and quality of life" is, at moderate certainty. Anything this system says must attach to the second sentence and not the first.

**None of that evidence is about generated music.** The autism music-therapy literature is live, relational and therapist-delivered. No review, meta-analysis or trial isolates pre-composed or machine-generated music in autism, and there is no subgroup to point at. The only non-live audio with a decent evidence base is rhythmic auditory stimulation, which is a sensorimotor entrainment prosthesis rather than an expressive one, and even there the effect reverses sign with tempo: Ghai's meta-analysis reports a positive effect of 0.70 for fast cueing and minus 0.24 for slow. Across all health applications, AI-generated music totals eight studies and 1,267 participants, four of them randomised, half rated weak, none in autism. The system therefore inherits nothing. It starts at zero, and the honest papers will say so in their first paragraph.

**The mechanism that would justify a musical channel is real, and the objection to it is equally real.** Allen, Davis and Hill found that physiological responsiveness to music was intact in autistic participants while verbal report of the response was reduced, and that the gap was fully mediated by alexithymia. That is precisely the shape of a warrant: the response exists and verbal articulation is the bottleneck. Alexithymia, difficulty identifying and describing one's own feelings, occurs in about half of autistic people, 49.93 per cent against 4.89 per cent in Kinnaird's review, described there as common rather than universal. But Suslow and Kersting found that alexithymia degrades the perception of emotion in music too. So the half of the population who most need an externalisation channel may be the least able to read what comes out of it. That is not a caveat to put in a limitations section. It is the primary empirical question, and the design has to answer it rather than hope.

Music is also not a clean strength. It is the smallest-difference modality. Heaton and Quintin found no deficit in musical emotion recognition, Sivathasan found an autistic advantage, and Leung's meta-analysis across seventy-two papers and 1,868 autistic participants found moderate deficits for fear and sadness specifically. The defensible sentence is that music is where the difference is smallest, not that it is a preserved strength.

**The sensory constraint is hard, quantified, and in the population's own words.** Hyperacusis affects 41.4 per cent currently and 60.6 per cent over a lifetime in a sample of 13,093, and half to seventy per cent report decreased sound tolerance at some point. Hyperacusis and misophonia need different mitigations. In autistic adults' own accounts of sensory experience, control is a first-order theme. Any audio this system produces for an autistic listener is subject to that before it is subject to anything else.

## 3. The Lacan problem, and what to do about it

This is the part that would sink the application if it went unaddressed. Psychoanalysis carries the most damaging episode in the history of autism: the refrigerator-mother account, which blamed parents and was refuted decades ago but did real harm while it stood. France is the live case rather than the historical one. The Haute Autorité de Santé adopted a new recommendation on 8 January 2026 that names psychoanalysis and packing under interventions that are not recommended, methods without evidence, with insufficient evidence, or ineffective in autism. That hardens the 2012 position, which had used the softer language of non-consensual methods. The documentary litigation ran the other way on appeal, with the Douai court in January 2014 overturning the ban on Sophie Robert's film against three Lacanian plaintiffs, one of whom is the author of the best-known Lacanian book on autism, which makes that source a conflicted one.

The Lacanian account itself, in Maleval's presentation, treats autism as a structure distinct from psychosis and does not blame parents, which is a genuine difference from Bettelheim. But absence of blame is not repudiation, and no review, trial, guideline or health-technology assessment anywhere supports a psychoanalytically framed intervention for autistic people. The literature that exists is theoretical and case-based, which is publication rather than evidence.

One trap is worth naming because it is tempting and it fails. It is not available to argue that behavioural approaches are criticised too, so everyone is in the same position. The French guidance recommends developmental and behavioural interventions while declining to recommend psychoanalysis, and the autistic community's critique of applied behaviour analysis is a critique of normalisation goals, not of evidence standards. Those two criticisms are not allies and cannot be made to cover for each other.

The answer is a split, and it is a clean one. The Lacanian vocabulary is the provenance of the state variables and belongs in the theory paper, where it is accurate and where the drama application makes it an asset, since Lacan wrote seminars on two plays already in the play library. It does not belong anywhere near a clinical or user-facing layer. This is not concealment. The mathematics does not depend on Lacan being clinically right about anything: the three registers are a three-way decomposition of a state, and renaming them changes no output. Declaring the provenance where it is true and not carrying it where it does no work is simply accurate about what does the work. The practical consequence is that the drama papers and any therapy paper must present the theory differently. Same machine, two provenances.

A second consequence follows from the participatory literature. The AASPIRE guidelines hold that instruments validated in general populations are not valid for autistic adults without participatory adaptation and psychometric retesting, which applies directly to the personality inventories inside the state vector. And on language, Kenny's survey of 3,470 people found 61 per cent of autistic adults preferring identity-first terms, with professionals the outlier group, and the authors' own conclusion was to ask rather than to mandate.

## 4. Five use cases, ordered by what they have to prove

**The therapist's composition tool.** A music therapist needs a piece with specified structural properties for a session: a tempo trajectory, a bounded dynamic range, a predictable form, a motif that returns. Generating it from a parameterised description is faster and far more controllable than searching for a recording. No health claim is made, no state is inferred about anyone, and the tool sits inside a credentialed professional's own practice, which the American Music Therapy Association's scope explicitly permits for professionals using music within their own competence. This is not a medical device, it needs no evidence to exist, and it is shippable now. It is also the use case that most closely matches what the software already does.

**The composed state, which is the interesting one.** Instead of the system inferring a person's state and playing it back, the person adjusts the parameters until the music matches how they feel, and the resulting piece is the shared object the session works with. The inversion changes everything about the tool's standing. Nothing is inferred about a person, so emotion-inference regulation does not engage. Nothing is pronounced, so the alexithymia objection changes shape: the client is not being told what they feel and then failing to read it, they are searching a space and stopping when it fits, which is a recognition task rather than a production task and is exactly the operation alexithymia impairs least. Allen's finding supplies the mechanism, that the response is intact while the words are the bottleneck, and this design gives a medium that is not words. It also fits the double-empathy framing, where the tool is a medium both parties work inside rather than a translation of one party for the other, though that literature is not unanimous and should be cited carefully.

**Longitudinal reflection.** The same declared states across sessions, rendered as a progression a client and therapist can listen back to. Same posture as above, no inference, no claim. Worth noting that this is the README's own original framing of the therapeutic idea.

**The text route, which needs the least new engineering.** The system's working pipeline is text in, state derived, score out. A client's own written scene, narrative or journal entry is a text. That lands the application in drama therapy and expressive-writing territory, both of which are established modalities, and it requires no new input mechanism at all. It is the shortest path from what is built to what a therapist could use on Monday.

**Sensory-bounded generation, which is the one genuine technical contribution.** Because the music is generated from parameters rather than selected from recordings, its acoustic properties can be guaranteed rather than hoped for: a ceiling on level, a bound on the rate of dynamic change, no content above a declared frequency, no unprepared dissonance, a predictable form, a stop control that works instantly and without penalty. Recorded music cannot offer that; a generator can prove it. Set against hyperacusis prevalence and the centrality of control in autistic adults' own accounts, a generator with provable acoustic bounds is a real contribution, it requires no clinical claim, and I did not find anyone making it. If one thing from this domain deserves to be a paper on its own, it is this.

## 5. The risk that is specific to this tool

The obvious risks are physical and manageable: loudness, sudden onsets, aversive spectral content, the sign reversal that Ghai's tempo result illustrates so cleanly. The risk that is specific to this system is epistemic. A person whose difficulty is precisely in identifying their own internal state, handed a confident-looking external representation of that state produced by an unvalidated mapping, may take the representation as the answer. The tool would then not be supporting articulation but overwriting it, and it would do so most strongly in exactly the people it is aimed at. I did not find this stated in the literature; it is my inference from placing the alexithymia work next to the tool's design, and it should be presented as such rather than cited to someone else. The design answer is the direction reversal in use case two. A system that asks is safe in a way that a system that pronounces is not.

## 6. The study that would settle it

Can autistic adults with high alexithymia represent their state more consistently in a musical parameter space than in words? The measure is test-retest consistency, not accuracy, because there is no gold standard for someone's internal state and pretending otherwise is the error the whole design is trying to avoid. Participants describe a remembered situation verbally and compose it musically, twice, separated by enough time; the comparison is the stability of each representation, with alexithymia score as the moderator. The prediction that would make the tool worth building is that musical consistency holds up where verbal consistency degrades with alexithymia. The result that would kill it is that both degrade together, which is what Suslow and Kersting's finding predicts. It is runnable, it is falsifiable, it needs no device pathway because nothing is claimed to treat anything, and it would be the first evidence the system has ever had.

## 7. The regulatory line, concretely

Device status turns on claims, not on technology. The Food and Drug Administration's general wellness guidance uses, as its own worked example of a claim that is not general wellness, a claim that a computer game will diagnose or treat autism. In Europe, Rule 11 of Annex VIII puts therapeutic decision-support software at class IIa or above, which means a notified body. Adjunct status is not a shelter: the one autism product with a United States authorisation is authorised as an adjunct to diagnosis and is still a class II prescription device, and the best-known prescription digital therapeutic needed 348 participants against an active digital control and came away with a claim narrowed to improving attention as measured by computer-based testing rather than treating anything. No music-generation product has been authorised in autism or mental health.

So the permitted sentences are about composition, about supporting a professional's own practice, and about general wellbeing without naming a condition. The forbidden sentences name autism, name a symptom, or promise a change in one. A tool that composes music from a description a therapist or a client supplies, and says nothing about what listening to it will do, stays a music tool.

## 8. Where this should sit in the series

Not inside the four agreed papers. The theory, mathematics, mapping and application papers are about the instrument and its dramatic use, and folding a clinical domain into them would bury it and would force the Lacanian frame into a setting where it is a liability. This is a fifth paper, written in a different register: identity-first language, the null trial in the opening paragraph, no theoretical vocabulary that the domain has ruled out, no outcome claims, and the sensory constraints as requirements rather than recommendations. If the sensory-bounded generation idea holds up on closer inspection it may deserve a sixth, since it is the one contribution here that is technical, novel and needs no clinical evidence at all.
