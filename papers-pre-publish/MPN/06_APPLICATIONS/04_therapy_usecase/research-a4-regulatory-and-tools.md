# a4 — Regulatory status, evidence standards, and comparable tools for a system that generates music from a described psychological state

Scope note: this is a desk review of regulator and primary sources. Every citation is marked VERIFIED (document fetched and read in this session) or UNVERIFIED (seen only as a search result, or a claim I could not confirm against the primary text). Nothing below invents a rule number, clearance number, date or quotation. Where a fetch returned only part of a document, I say so rather than filling the gap.

## Findings

### 1. Medical device status

**EU — the definition is about purpose, not technology.** MDCG 2019-11, *Guidance on Qualification and Classification of Software in Regulation (EU) 2017/745 – MDR and Regulation (EU) 2017/746 – IVDR*, October 2019, reproduces the MDR Article 2(1) definition: a medical device is "any instrument, apparatus, appliance, software, implant, reagent, material or other article intended by the manufacturer to be used, alone or in combination, for human beings for one or more of the following specific medical purposes: diagnosis, prevention, monitoring, prediction, prognosis, treatment or alleviation of disease…" [MDCG-2019-11 — VERIFIED]. Software is named in the definition itself; there is no separate "software exemption." The guidance defines Medical Device Software (MDSW) as "software that is intended to be used, alone or in combination, for a purpose as specified in the definition of a 'medical device' in the MDR or IVDR" [MDCG-2019-11 — VERIFIED]. The operative word in both is *intended*: the manufacturer's stated intended purpose and claims decide qualification, not the sophistication of the model, not whether it uses AI, and not whether a clinician is in the room.

The same guidance draws the negative boundary explicitly: "Software intended for non-medical purposes (excluding MDR Annex XVI devices), such as invoicing or staff planning, does not qualify as a medical device software," and lists "e-mailing, web or voice messaging, data parsing, word processing, and back-up" as non-medical tasks [MDCG-2019-11 — VERIFIED].

**EU classification — Annex VIII Rule 11.** As reproduced in MDCG 2019-11: Rule 11 sub-rule (a) — "Software intended to provide information which is used to take decisions with diagnosis or therapeutic purposes is classified as class IIa, except if such decisions … may cause: death or an irreversible deterioration of a person's state of health [class III]; or a serious deterioration … or a surgical intervention [class IIb]." Sub-rule (b) covers software for monitoring vital physiological processes, class IIb where variations could result in immediate danger. Sub-rule (c): "All other software is classified as class I" [MDCG-2019-11 — VERIFIED]. Practical consequence: a music-generation tool that qualifies as a device at all will almost certainly land in class IIa under 11(a) if it feeds any therapeutic decision — which means a Notified Body, not self-certification. Class I self-certification is only available if the software qualifies as a device but is not decision-supporting, which is a narrow and contestable position.

**EU — wellbeing and Annex XVI.** The MDCG 2019-11 text I fetched does not address "wellbeing" or "lifestyle" software under those terms; the guidance addresses non-medical *purposes* generically [MDCG-2019-11 — VERIFIED, as a negative finding]. MDR Annex XVI creates a separate regime for listed groups of products *without* an intended medical purpose (the European Commission maintains a page on "Manufacturers of devices without an intended medical purpose," and MDCG 2023-5 addresses this class) [EC-ANNEXXVI, MDCG-2023-5 — UNVERIFIED, not fetched]. Two things matter for a music tool: (i) Annex XVI is a *closed list* of product groups, and I did not verify that any audio/software category appears on it — the working assumption should be that generative music is **not** an Annex XVI product; (ii) therefore a wellbeing music tool in the EU sits *outside* the MDR entirely, governed instead by consumer protection, the Unfair Commercial Practices regime, GDPR, and (for AI systems) the AI Act — not by a lighter medical framework. There is no "wellness device" MDR category to fall back into; it is device or not-device.

**United States — SaMD, the general wellness policy, and Cures Act section 3060.** FDA's guidance *General Wellness: Policy for Low Risk Devices* (document fetched; the version returned is dated January 6, 2026) defines a general wellness product as one that meets two factors: "(1) are intended for only general wellness use, as defined in this guidance, and (2) present a low risk to the safety of users and other persons" [FDA-GW — VERIFIED]. It sets out two categories of general wellness intended use. Category 1 covers claims about "sustaining general improvement to functions associated with health" without reference to diseases or conditions, and names as acceptable areas "weight management, physical fitness, including products intended for recreational use, relaxation or stress management, mental acuity, self-esteem, sleep management, or sexual function" [FDA-GW — VERIFIED]. Category 2 covers intended uses that "promote, track, and/or encourage choice(s), which, as part of a healthy lifestyle, may help to reduce the risk of certain chronic diseases or conditions" or "may help living well with certain chronic diseases or conditions" [FDA-GW — VERIFIED].

The same guidance states that a product is NOT general wellness if its labelling includes "1. references to specific diseases, clinical conditions, or diagnostic thresholds; 2. alerts, alarms, or prompts that recommend or require specific clinical action or medical management; 3. treatment guidance intended to inform or direct medical management; 4. claims of clinical equivalence, clinical accuracy, medical or clinical grade, or substitution for an FDA-authorized, cleared, or approved medical device; or 5. intended-use statements that explicitly target diagnosis, screening, monitoring, or management of a disease or condition" [FDA-GW — VERIFIED]. The low-risk screen asks whether the product is invasive, implanted, or "involves an intervention or technology that may pose a risk to the safety of users and other persons if specific regulatory controls are not applied" [FDA-GW — VERIFIED]. A generative audio app is non-invasive and non-implanted, so it passes the risk screen easily; the whole question for a US developer is category, i.e. claims.

Decisively for this project, the guidance gives as an example of a claim that is NOT general wellness: **"A claim that a computer game will diagnose or treat autism"** [FDA-GW — VERIFIED]. That is close to a direct ruling on the described product if it is marketed for autism outcomes. It also gives as NOT general wellness "a claim that a product will treat or diagnose obesity" and "a claim that a product will treat an eating disorder, such as anorexia," while listing as acceptable "claims to improve mental acuity, instruction following, concentration, problem-solving" [FDA-GW — VERIFIED]. Note the asymmetry: *improving concentration* is a permitted wellness claim; *treating a condition characterised by inattention* is not.

Section 3060 of the 21st Century Cures Act amended the FD&C Act to exclude certain software functions from the device definition, including general wellness software and a defined class of clinical decision support (CDS) software, and FDA revised its digital health guidances accordingly [CURES-3060 — UNVERIFIED, law firm commentary seen in search results only; I did not fetch the statutory text or FDA's CDS guidance]. The CDS exclusion is conditional in ways a generative-music product is unlikely to satisfy, because the statutory carve-out turns on the software enabling the healthcare professional "to independently review the basis" for its recommendations — an opaque generative model that outputs audio does not lend itself to that. Treat the CDS route as unavailable unless a specific legal opinion says otherwise. [Reasoning, not a cited rule.]

**The line, stated plainly.** In both jurisdictions the trigger is *intended purpose as evidenced by claims, labelling, marketing and instructions for use* — not the algorithm, not the data, not whether it is "AI." The same code base is a consumer wellbeing app or a class IIa device depending entirely on what the developer says it is for.

### 2. The practical test

Contrast the two authorised US products against the wellness examples:

- EndeavorRx's authorised indication is "EndeavorRx is a digital therapeutic indicated to improve attention function as measured by computer-based testing in children ages 8-12 years old with primarily inattentive or combined-type ADHD, who have a demonstrated attention issue" [FDA-DEN200026 — VERIFIED]. Note how narrow it is: *improve attention function as measured by computer-based testing*, not "treats ADHD." This is what a claim looks like after a regulator has constrained it to the endpoint that was actually measured.
- Cognoa's authorised indication is "intended for use by healthcare providers as an aid in the diagnosis of Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD) for patients ages 18 months through 72 months who are at risk for developmental delay based on concerns of a parent, caregiver, or healthcare provider," with the limitation that "the device is not intended for use as a stand-alone diagnostic device but as an adjunct to the diagnostic process" [FDA-DEN200069 — VERIFIED]. Even as an *adjunct*, and even with a clinician in the loop, it is a class II prescription device. "Adjunct" and "clinician-supervised" do not exempt a product from device status.

So "it only supports the therapist" is not a safe harbour when the support is diagnostic or therapeutic in content. The genuinely safe non-device territory is narrower: software that manages the *practice* rather than the *patient* (scheduling, session notes, billing, exporting a clip the therapist chose), and software making Category 1 general wellness claims about relaxation, focus or sleep with no disease reference.

Claim language that pushes a product across the line: naming a condition in the intended use; "therapeutic"; "clinically proven to reduce anxiety"; "reduces meltdowns"; "regulates sensory overload in autistic children"; "assesses"; "screens"; "monitors symptoms"; "recommends an intervention"; "clinical-grade"; dosage or prescription framing ("three sessions per week"); before/after symptom scores in marketing; and any alert that tells a clinician or carer to act.

### 3. Digital therapeutics evidence standards

**NICE Evidence Standards Framework (ESF) for digital health technologies.** The ESF sorts technologies into tiers by function and risk. As fetched: Tier A — "The technology is intended to release costs or staff time or to improve efficiency" (system service, no direct patient outcomes). Tier B — "The technology is intended to help patients and citizens to manage their own health and wellness" (communicating about health/care, health and care diaries, promoting good health). Tier C — technologies "intended to treat or diagnose a specific condition or guide treatment, diagnosis and care choices," or "intended to have direct health outcomes," or that are "a medical device or an in vitro diagnostic or a screening tool"; functions include inform clinical management, drive clinical management, diagnose a condition, treat a condition [NICE-ESF-USERGUIDE — VERIFIED]. The current ESF is organised as 21 evidence standards across five groups — design factors (standards 1–9), describing value (10–13), demonstrating performance (14–16), delivering value (17–18), deployment considerations (19–21). Standards 1–6 apply to all tiers; standards 7–9 apply only to tiers B and C; standard 14 is specific to tier C and is the clinical-effectiveness standard; standards 15–21 apply to all tiers [NICE-ESF-SECTIONC and NICE-ESF-USERGUIDE — VERIFIED]. A music-generation tool used in therapy is a Tier C technology the moment it claims to treat, or to guide a clinician's treatment choice, and standard 14 then applies.

I did not verify, against the current ESF text, the specific study designs NICE names under standard 14 (the older 2019 framework used "minimum" and "best practice" columns with high-quality intervention studies and RCTs at the top tier). Treat the precise design wording as UNVERIFIED. [NICE-ESF-2019-PDF — UNVERIFIED, not fetched.]

**Germany — DiGA (§139e SGB V fast track).** My attempt to fetch the BfArM guide returned only the landing page; BfArM's document is titled "The Fast-Track Process for Digital Health Applications (DiGA) according to Section 139e SGB V. A Guide for Manufacturers, Service Providers and Users," with a page date of 2020-08-07 and a note that "the current version of the guide is only available in German" [BFARM-DIGA — partially VERIFIED for title/date only]. The substantive requirements — that a DiGA must be a CE-marked medical device of risk class I or IIa; that the manufacturer must demonstrate a "positive healthcare effect" (*positiver Versorgungseffekt*), split into medical benefit and patient-relevant improvements of structure and procedure; that this must be shown by a comparative study; and that a provisional listing runs for twelve months with a possible extension while the trial runs — are **UNVERIFIED** here. Do not cite numbers for the DiGA pathway without fetching the German guide or §139e SGB V. Peer-reviewed analyses of DiGA evidence quality exist and would be the place to check (e.g. an npj Digital Medicine systematic review of DiGA healthcare effects and evidence robustness, and a BMC Health Services Research analysis of permanently listed DiGA) [DIGA-EVIDENCE-REVIEWS — UNVERIFIED].

**FDA expectations for prescription digital therapeutics.** The De Novo record for EndeavorRx shows what FDA actually required: a pivotal randomised trial, STARS-ADHD, N=348 children (180 EndeavorRx, 168 control), primary endpoint "change in the Test of Variables of Attention (TOVA) Attention Performance Index (API)," result "EndeavorRx showed a statistically significant improvement from baseline in the TOVA API compared to the digital control (0.93 vs 0.03; p=0.006)" [FDA-DEN200026 — VERIFIED]. Note the *active digital control* — a sham app, not a waitlist. For a music tool the analogue is a plausible non-generative or non-personalised audio control, which is a hard design problem because participants can often tell. The special controls established for this device type (21 CFR 882.5803, class II) include clinical performance testing using validated measures, software verification/validation and hazard analysis, and labelling warnings that the device is not standalone and not a substitute for medication [FDA-DEN200026 — VERIFIED].

### 4. Authorised or cleared examples

- **Cognoa ASD Diagnosis Aid ("Canvas Dx")** — De Novo DEN200069, June 2, 2021. Class II, 21 CFR 882.1491, new classification "Pediatric Autism Spectrum Disorder diagnosis aid," prescription device under 21 CFR 801.109. Cleared as an *aid* in the diagnosis of ASD for patients 18–72 months at risk of developmental delay, explicitly "not intended for use as a stand-alone diagnostic device but as an adjunct to the diagnostic process" [FDA-DEN200069 — VERIFIED]. It is a diagnostic aid; it treats nothing.
- **EndeavorRx (Akili)** — De Novo DEN200026 (request received April 16, 2020), class II, 21 CFR 882.5803, "Digital therapy device for Attention Deficit Hyperactivity Disorder." Authorised to improve *attention function as measured by computer-based testing* in children 8–12 with inattentive or combined-type ADHD [FDA-DEN200026 — VERIFIED].
- **MedRhythms InTandem (MR-001)** — the nearest music-based comparator. Per the company's own announcement, InTandem was listed with FDA as a class II device on July 6, 2023, "to improve walking and ambulation in adults with chronic stroke walking deficits," having earlier received Breakthrough Device Designation (2020), and having completed a pivotal trial in 2023 [MEDRHYTHMS-PR — VERIFIED as a company statement; NOT verified against an FDA database record]. The mechanism is Rhythmic Auditory Stimulation: "auditory-motor entrainment, through which the motor and auditory systems in the brain subconsciously synchronize to an external cue, such as music," delivered via "sensors, software, and music" [MEDRHYTHMS-PR — VERIFIED as a company statement]. Important caveat: an FDA *listing* is a registration act and is not the same as 510(k) clearance, De Novo authorisation or PMA approval; press coverage describing this as "FDA approval" is loose. The pathway should be confirmed in FDA's device databases before being relied on. I found no music-generation product with a device authorisation for an autism or mental-health indication.

### 5. Existing music tools in therapy and autism support

- **Endel.** Consumer generative soundscape app. Its science page says soundscapes are "personalized to your inputs at all times" and describes an engine called Endel Pacific, but does not, on that page, enumerate which physiological inputs are used [ENDEL-SCIENCE — VERIFIED]. Claims include helping users "focus, sleep, and relax," supporting "productivity throughout the day, adjusting to your energy levels," "×7 better, more consistent focus than productivity music playlists and working in silence," lowering cortisol and activating the parasympathetic nervous system, a "Pavlovian-like sound effect" for sleep onset, and sound masking [ENDEL-SCIENCE — VERIFIED]. Studies listed include a 2022 focus study (Haruvi et al., *Frontiers in Computational Neuroscience*, BCI-measured focus), a 2022 ADHD white-noise study by Lin et al. in preschoolers, and 2023 sleep research associated with a European Commission/CORDIS project; sample sizes and effect sizes are not given on the page, and most of the remaining footnotes are general music-science citations rather than product studies [ENDEL-SCIENCE — VERIFIED]. Notably, the page carries **no** "not a medical device" disclaimer [ENDEL-SCIENCE — VERIFIED as a negative finding]. The cortisol and parasympathetic claims are physiological-effect claims and sit closer to the line than the focus/sleep claims; the white-noise-and-ADHD citation is a condition reference, which is exactly the kind of adjacency FDA's general wellness guidance warns about.
- **Brain.fm.** Functional music for focus, marketed as science-backed, with a press release announcing a peer-reviewed study of its ADHD-oriented focus music [BRAINFM-PR — UNVERIFIED, not fetched]. Input is user-selected mental state (focus/relax/sleep) rather than physiology, as far as I could establish without fetching.
- **AffectMachine (Agres and colleagues).** Research systems that generate affective music in real time and are explicitly designed for biosignal-driven / neurofeedback use: *AffectMachine-Classical: a novel system for generating affective classical music* (arXiv:2304.04915; indexed in PubMed) and *AffectMachine-Pop: A controllable expert system for real-time pop music generation* (arXiv:2506.08200) [AFFECTMACHINE — UNVERIFIED, abstracts seen in search results only]. These are the closest research analogue to "music generated from a described psychological state," and they are positioned as research/wellbeing systems, not regulated devices.
- **Music therapy and autism research systems.** Recent literature includes a focused survey of generative-AI-based music therapy systems (Applied Sciences, doi 10.3390/app16094120), work on AI-assisted music therapy tools in mental health interventions (Frontiers in Psychology, 2026), an intelligent adaptive reinforcement-learning music therapy system for children with autism (Springer chapter), and a TL-GCN/deep-learning method for enhancing social skills in autistic children (Scientific Reports, 2025) [MT-AI-LIT — UNVERIFIED, titles from search results only]. These show an active research field with essentially no regulated products in it: the gap between published prototypes and authorised devices is the whole commercial and ethical space this project sits in.
- **Established music-therapy practice software** (session documentation, assessment scales, library tools used by credentialed music therapists) is generally practice-support software and non-device, but I did not verify any named product in this session [MT-PRACTICE-SOFTWARE — UNVERIFIED].

Pattern across the commercial products: inputs are shallow (self-selected state, time of day, sometimes heart rate); claims are wellbeing-shaped (focus, relax, sleep) and stop short of naming a condition in the intended-use statement even where marketing cites condition-specific literature; published evidence is typically one or two studies with proxy endpoints (EEG-derived focus indices, self-report) rather than clinical outcomes. None of them would satisfy NICE Tier C standard 14 or an FDA De Novo.

### 6. Research ethics and data

The following are the expected requirements; the specific instruments are UNVERIFIED in this session and should be confirmed against primary text before being relied on in a protocol.

- **EU ethics.** A study with autistic participants requires prior favourable opinion from a competent research ethics committee under national law, with particular attention to capacity, proportionate assent/consent, and the involvement of legal representatives for minors or adults lacking capacity. Where the study evaluates the tool's performance for a medical purpose, MDR clinical investigation requirements and national competent authority notification may also engage. [EU-ETHICS — UNVERIFIED.]
- **US ethics.** Human-subjects research requires IRB review under the Common Rule (45 CFR part 46), with subpart D protections if children are enrolled, and FDA's own human-subject regulations (21 CFR parts 50 and 56) if the study supports a device submission. Autistic participants are not a categorical "vulnerable population" under the Common Rule, but capacity-to-consent assessment and assent procedures are expected. [US-ETHICS — UNVERIFIED, no primary text fetched.]
- **Is generated audio health data?** Under GDPR, "data concerning health" is defined in Article 4(15) and elaborated in Recital 35, and is a special category under Article 9 [GDPR-ART4-15, GDPR-REC35, GDPR-ART9 — UNVERIFIED, not fetched]. The analysis that matters: the *user's described psychological state* is plainly personal data revealing information about mental health, and in a therapy context is health data. The *generated audio itself* is derived from that input, so where the audio is stored linked to the individual and its parameters allow the state to be inferred, it should be treated as health data too — the safer and defensible position. If the audio is stored with the generation prompt or state parameters, there is no serious argument it is not health data. Research processing then needs an Article 9(2) condition, most commonly 9(2)(j) scientific research with Member State safeguards, or explicit consent under 9(2)(a). A DPIA should be assumed necessary. [Reasoning on top of UNVERIFIED sources.]
- **Registration.** ICMJE requires prospective registration of clinical trials in a public registry as a condition of publication [ICMJE-REG — UNVERIFIED, not fetched]. In practice: register on ClinicalTrials.gov (US, and accepted internationally) or ISRCTN (UK/EU common), *before enrolment of the first participant*, and publish the protocol and statistical analysis plan in advance (a protocol paper, or a registry attachment, or OSF/PROSPERO for a systematic review component). For an interventional study of a therapeutic claim, prospective registration is not optional if the results are ever to be published or cited in a regulatory submission.

## The line a generated-music tool must not cross, and what it may say instead

- **Do not name a condition in the intended-use statement.** FDA's general wellness guidance lists "a claim that a computer game will diagnose or treat autism" as an example of a claim that is not general wellness [FDA-GW — VERIFIED]. Forbidden: "music therapy for autism," "reduces autistic meltdowns," "supports emotional regulation in autistic children." Permitted: "generates personalised soundscapes for relaxation and focus," with no population named in the intended use.
- **Do not claim treatment, alleviation, diagnosis, prevention, monitoring, prediction or prognosis of disease.** Those seven words are the MDR Article 2(1) medical purposes [MDCG-2019-11 — VERIFIED]; using any of them about a condition qualifies the software as a device in the EU. Forbidden: "alleviates anxiety." Permitted: "for relaxation and stress management" — a Category 1 general wellness area named by FDA [FDA-GW — VERIFIED].
- **Do not describe output as therapy, dose or prescription.** Forbidden: "a 20-minute therapeutic session, three times weekly"; "prescribe to your client." Permitted: "a soundscape you can play for as long as you like"; "a tool a music therapist may choose to use within their own session."
- **Do not output a recommendation, alert or prompt directing clinical action.** FDA lists "alerts, alarms, or prompts that recommend or require specific clinical action or medical management" and "treatment guidance intended to inform or direct medical management" as disqualifying [FDA-GW — VERIFIED]. Forbidden: "state score is elevated — increase session frequency." Permitted: showing the user their own inputs back with no interpretation or instruction.
- **Do not report or infer a clinical state, score or threshold.** Forbidden: "your anxiety level is severe"; any numeric index framed against a clinical cut-off; "references to … diagnostic thresholds" is expressly disqualifying [FDA-GW — VERIFIED]. Permitted: free-text or mood-word input used only to steer musical parameters, with the tool never asserting what that means clinically.
- **Do not claim clinical grade or equivalence to an authorised device.** FDA names "claims of clinical equivalence, clinical accuracy, medical or clinical grade, or substitution for an FDA-authorized, cleared, or approved medical device" as disqualifying [FDA-GW — VERIFIED]. Forbidden: "clinical-grade adaptive audio"; "an alternative to music therapy." Permitted: "for general wellbeing; not a medical device and not a substitute for professional care."
- **"Adjunct" and "clinician-supervised" are not exemptions.** Cognoa's product is an *aid* used by healthcare providers and is nonetheless a class II prescription device [FDA-DEN200069 — VERIFIED]. Forbidden framing: "it's only an adjunct, so it isn't a device." Permitted: genuine practice-support functions — scheduling, session logging, exporting audio the therapist selected — which sit outside the patient-facing claim entirely.
- **Keep marketing copy inside the intended-use statement.** Qualification looks at claims wherever they appear, including web copy, app-store text, testimonials and conference talks. A compliant IFU plus a blog post saying "reduces autistic children's distress" is a non-compliant product. Forbidden: condition-specific case studies used as promotion. Permitted: publishing research with explicit "investigational; not a claim about the marketed product" framing.
- **Cite condition-specific literature with care.** Endel's page cites an ADHD white-noise study alongside product claims [ENDEL-SCIENCE — VERIFIED]; that adjacency invites a regulator to read a condition claim into a wellness product. Permitted: cite mechanism literature (entrainment, arousal, attention) without tying it to a diagnosed population.
- **If you do want a clinical claim, plan for the real bar.** EndeavorRx needed a randomised pivotal trial, N=348, against an *active digital control*, on a validated endpoint (TOVA API, 0.93 vs 0.03, p=0.006) [FDA-DEN200026 — VERIFIED]; NICE would treat the tool as Tier C with standard 14 clinical effectiveness applying [NICE-ESF — VERIFIED]. Anything less supports a wellbeing product only.
- **Constrain the claim to what was measured.** The authorised EndeavorRx wording is "improve attention function *as measured by computer-based testing*," not "treats ADHD" [FDA-DEN200026 — VERIFIED]. If a trial is ever run here, expect the permitted claim to be similarly narrow, e.g. "improves [named measure] scores," not "improves wellbeing in autism."
- **Treat the state description and the generated audio as health data by default.** Store them separately from identifiers, run a DPIA, obtain an Article 9(2) basis, get ethics approval before any participant is enrolled, and register the trial prospectively on ClinicalTrials.gov or ISRCTN with a published protocol [GDPR, ICMJE — UNVERIFIED, but this is the conservative default].

## Gaps

1. **MDCG 2019-11 on wellbeing.** The fetched text did not address "wellbeing" or "lifestyle" software under those terms. The guidance's decision-step figures and its treatment of software driving/influencing a device were not captured. Re-fetch the PDF directly and read the qualification decision steps and section on modules.
2. **MDR Annex XVI and Recital 19.** Not fetched. I could not confirm whether any category relevant to audio or software appears on the Annex XVI closed list, nor read the MDR recital dealing with software. Confirm before stating the EU "no medical purpose" position.
3. **DiGA.** The substantive BfArM requirements (class I/IIa limit, positive healthcare effect and its two limbs, comparative-study requirement, twelve-month provisional listing) are **unverified**. The English guide appears to be superseded by a German-only current version. Fetch §139e SGB V and the current German guide, or the DiGA Ordinance (DiGAV).
4. **Cures Act section 3060 and the CDS guidance.** Only law-firm commentary was seen. The statutory exclusions and FDA's *Clinical Decision Support Software* guidance need to be read directly before relying on any CDS argument.
5. **NICE standard 14 detail.** The specific study designs NICE expects under standard 14, and any minimum/best-practice split in the current ESF, were not captured.
6. **MedRhythms InTandem pathway.** The company says "FDA listing" and class II; press coverage says "approval." The actual regulatory route (510(k), De Novo, or exempt-and-listed) should be confirmed in FDA's 510(k)/De Novo/registration-and-listing databases. This matters a lot, because InTandem is the precedent a music-based product would cite.
7. **No music-generation device authorisation in autism or mental health was found.** Absence of evidence here, not evidence of absence — a targeted search of FDA's device databases and EUDAMED would be needed to confirm.
8. **Brain.fm, music-therapy practice software, and the AI-music-therapy research papers** were not fetched; their inputs, claims and evidence are characterised from titles and snippets only.
9. **GDPR, Common Rule, ICMJE primary texts** were not fetched. The health-data characterisation of generated audio is my reasoning, not a cited regulator position; no regulator guidance specifically on generative-audio-as-health-data was located.
10. **AI Act.** Not researched at all. An EU-market tool in a therapeutic or emotion-inference context may face obligations there independent of the MDR — emotion recognition in particular is a flagged area. This is a material open question.
