# a3 — Autism, music and internal state: what the cognitive and perceptual science supports, and what it warns against

Scope note: this answers one question — whether there is a scientific basis for using a *musical* channel to represent a described psychological state, with autistic people as a population of interest, and what the risks are. Identity-first language ("autistic people") is used throughout as the majority community preference; preference varies and some people prefer person-first language.

Verification convention: **VERIFIED** = I retrieved the publisher page, open-access PDF, or the indexed abstract record and read the numbers reported below. **UNVERIFIED** = I have the citation but did not read the source in this session; no numbers are attributed to it.

## Findings

### 1. Emotion recognition in music versus in faces and voices

This is the load-bearing claim and it is **weaker and more contested than the popular summary suggests**. The honest statement is: *musical emotion recognition is the modality on which autistic–non-autistic differences are smallest and most often absent, but "preserved" is not the same as "intact for all emotions", and the meta-analytic evidence base for music specifically is thin.*

**The founding study.** Heaton, Hermelin and Pring (1999) tested **14 children with autism and Asperger syndrome against 14 age- and IQ-matched controls** on whether major- and minor-mode melodic fragments matched a schematic happy or sad face. "The groups did not differ in their ability to ascribe the musical examples to the two affective categories," and the authors concluded that "in contrast to their performance within social and interpersonal domains, children with autistic disorders showed no deficits in processing affect in musical stimuli." [Heaton1999 — VERIFIED]. This is a small sample, two emotion categories, a binary forced choice, and a mode-based cue that is close to a perceptual rule rather than an affective inference. It should be read as an existence proof, not as a strong effect.

**The most-cited replication and extension.** Quintin, Bhatara, Poissant, Fombonne and Levitin (2011) tested **26 high-functioning autistic adolescents and 26 typically developing adolescents**, matched on performance IQ, auditory working memory and musical training, on four-way musical emotion categorisation (happy, sad, scared, peaceful). "When verbal IQ was controlled for, there was no significant effect of diagnostic group." The autistic group also rated emotional intensity similarly and showed appropriate metacognitive calibration — greater confidence on correct than incorrect trials [Quintin2011 — VERIFIED]. Note the conditional: *when verbal IQ was controlled*. The unadjusted comparison is not a null.

**The strongest direct three-way comparison.** Sivathasan, Dahary, Burack and Quintin (2023) is the one study that puts music, faces and voices in the same participants. **25 autistic children (19 boys, ages 9–13, M = 11) and 23 typically developing children (11 boys, ages 6–12, M = 9.7)**, using the Musical Emotional Bursts (24 melodies), NimStim faces (24), and Montreal Affective Voices (24), for happy, sad and fear.
- Autistic group **more accurate than TD group on music**: F(1,45) = 5.40, p = .025, ηp² = .11.
- **No group difference on faces**: F(1,45) = .94, p = .34, ηp² = .02.
- **No group difference on voices**: F(1,45) = .22, p = .64, ηp² = .005.
- Within-group profile: TD children were better with faces than music (p < .001); autistic children performed equally well with music and faces (p = .36). Both groups were most accurate with voices (AS M = .92, TD M = .91).
- Dimensional ratings: autistic children rated happy stimuli as significantly *more positive* (F(1,45) = 11.77, p = .001, ηp² = .21); no group differences for sad or fear valence, and none for arousal.
[Sivathasan2023 — VERIFIED]
Important caveat the authors themselves flag: this study found **no facial or vocal deficit at all**, contrary to meta-analytic expectation. So its "music advantage" is a *relative* advantage over a baseline that was already typical — it does not, on its own, demonstrate that music rescues an otherwise impaired channel. Groups also differed on chronological age, verbal mental age and SRS-2.

**The contrary meta-analytic finding — the most important counterweight in this section.** Leung, Sin, Dawson, Ong, Zhao, Veić and Liu (2022) meta-analysed **72 papers, 1,868 autistic and 2,232 neurotypical participants, 259 accuracy datasets**. Their music results are *not* uniformly null:
- Music, **fear**: g = −0.50 (moderate deficit).
- Music, **sadness**: g = −0.63 (moderate deficit).
- Music, **happiness**: non-significant.
- For comparison — human faces: anger g = −0.47, fear −0.47, happiness −0.47, sadness −0.48, disgust −0.40, surprise −0.23, composite −0.72. Speech prosody: anger −0.34, happiness −0.52, disgust −0.93; fear, sadness and composite non-significant. Non-human faces: no significant differences (anger g = −0.11, happiness g = 0.34).
- Overall composite across all domains: g = −0.77; after trim-and-fill for publication bias, composite g = −0.53 and fear g = −0.44, both still significant.
- Moderators: IQ did **not** moderate group differences in the main analyses (one exception: higher verbal IQ predicted *larger* differences for happiness). Age did moderate the composite — older autistic participants showed larger differences. Task demand mattered: **verbal** response tasks produced significant group differences across all emotion types, whereas **non-verbal** tasks showed impairment only for disgust.
- Heterogeneity was high (I² ≥ 50%) for anger, fear, happiness, sadness and composite.
- The authors nonetheless write that "recognition of musical emotions seems unimpaired in ASD," while noting the music evidence rests on **only 2–4 datasets per emotion**.
[Leung2021/2022 — VERIFIED. Journal attribution needs care: the open-access author PDF is filed as *Developmental Review* (ScienceDirect PII S0273229721000551, ISSN 0273-2297 = Developmental Review), but the fetched text rendered the running head as *Research in Autism Spectrum Disorders*. Treat the journal name as provisionally *Developmental Review* and re-check before citing.]

This is the single most decision-relevant tension in the literature: **the meta-analysis finds music deficits for exactly the two negative emotions (fear, sadness) that a state-representation tool would most need to render, on a very small number of datasets, while individual studies find music null or advantaged.** The "music is the preserved channel" claim cannot be made unconditionally.

**Physiological versus verbal response — the finding most directly relevant to a tool.** Allen, Davis and Hill (2013) compared high-functioning autistic adults with matched controls on physiological and verbal responses to music. "The groups did not differ significantly on physiological responsiveness, but the autism group was significantly lower on the verbal measure. However, inclusion of the alexithymia score as a mediator variable nullified this group difference, suggesting that the difference was due not to absence of underlying emotional responsiveness to music in autism, but to a reduced ability to articulate it." [Allen2013 — VERIFIED]. This is the strongest single result supporting a non-verbal externalisation channel: the *response* is there, the *verbal report* is the bottleneck, and the bottleneck is alexithymia.

**Qualitative/self-report.** Allen, Hill and Heaton (2009) conducted semi-structured interviews with **12 high-functioning autistic adults**. Most "exploit music for a wide range of purposes in the cognitive, emotional and social domains," but their descriptions of mood states "reflected a greater reliance on internally focused (arousal) rather than externally focused (emotive) language" compared with studies of typically developing people [Allen2009 — VERIFIED]. Design implication: autistic listeners may find **arousal/energy language more natural than discrete-emotion labels**. Small, self-selected, verbally fluent sample.

**Neuroimaging.** Caria, Venuti and de Falco (2011, *Cerebral Cortex*) scanned autistic and neurotypical participants listening to happy and sad music. Autistic participants "activated cortical and subcortical brain regions known to be involved in emotion processing and reward," but showed **decreased activity in premotor area and left anterior insula, especially for happy music** [Caria2011 — VERIFIED]. Read carefully this is a *mixed* result — the reward and emotion circuitry engages, but the insula (interoceptive/affective-integration hub, and the region implicated in alexithymia) engages less. It is evidence for preserved *engagement* and altered *integration*, not for identity of processing. Sample size not reported in the abstract record I retrieved.

**Intervention literature (separate question, weaker evidence).** Music-based intervention reviews consistently report small-to-moderate benefits with poor methodological quality. Navarro et al. (2025) screened 346 publications, included 120, meta-analysed 15, and reported **z = 1.89, p = .06 for social interaction; z = 2.93, p = .01 for vowel articulation; z = 1.92, p = .06 for behaviour after outlier removal; z = 1.67, p = .09 for quality of life** — i.e. mostly non-significant at conventional thresholds [Navarro2025 — VERIFIED]. Applewhite et al. (2022) reviewed 81 studies (43,353 participants) and concluded RCT-quality evidence "was scarce", with small samples and heterogeneous methods [Applewhite2022 — VERIFIED]. A transdiagnostic meta-review of music therapy across psychiatry found pooled SMDs of 0.57 (depression), 0.47 (anxiety), 0.47 (quality of life), but **effects were not maintained at follow-up and all results were low or very low GRADE evidence** [Lassner2024 — VERIFIED]. A large (n = 200) improvisational music therapy RCT for autistic children is in progress [Jaschke2024 — VERIFIED as protocol]. The earlier large multi-site TIME-A trial reported broadly null primary outcomes [UNVERIFIED — cited from memory; do not quote figures without retrieval].

### 2. Alexithymia

**Definition and measurement.** Alexithymia is "a reduced ability to identify and describe one's own emotion" [Bird2013 — VERIFIED], canonically covering difficulty identifying feelings, difficulty describing feelings, and externally oriented thinking. The dominant instrument is the 20-item Toronto Alexithymia Scale (TAS-20), a self-report measure; the Bermond–Vorst Alexithymia Questionnaire is the main alternative [Bird2013 — VERIFIED]. The reliance on *self-report about self-knowledge* is a structural weakness that recurs below.

**Prevalence.** Bird and Cook (2013) state roughly **10% in the general population and 40–65% of autistic adults meeting criteria**, without giving underlying sample sizes [Bird2013 — VERIFIED]. The best meta-analytic estimate is Kinnaird, Stewart and Tchanturia (2019), *European Psychiatry* 55, 80–89: **15 articles comparing autistic and neurotypical groups; alexithymia prevalence 49.93% in the autistic group versus 4.89% in the neurotypical group**, with autistic participants scoring significantly higher on all TAS scores. Their explicit conclusion: alexithymia is "**common, rather than universal**, in ASD", and co-occurring autism-plus-alexithymia likely represents a specific subgroup [Kinnaird2019 — VERIFIED, full text].

**The alexithymia hypothesis.** Bird and Cook's claim is that "where observed, emotional impairments are due to alexithymia — a condition that frequently co-occurs with autism — rather than a feature of autism *per se*." Their supporting evidence as presented in the paper: Cook et al. found alexithymia, not autism severity, predicted attribution thresholds for emotional faces; Bird et al. found empathic anterior-insula activity was predicted by alexithymia in both autistic and non-autistic participants, with no autism group difference once alexithymia was controlled [Bird2013 — VERIFIED].

**Evidence for.** Allen, Davis and Hill (2013), above — the autistic-vs-control difference in *verbal* response to music was nullified by alexithymia as mediator [VERIFIED]. Keating, Kraaijkamp and Cook (2026), **58 autistic and 59 non-autistic adults** matched on age, sex and non-verbal reasoning: "We found **no group differences** in emotional consistency, emotion differentiation, and understanding or differentiation of emotion concepts **after controlling for alexithymia**" [Keating2026a — VERIFIED].

**Evidence against / partial.** Heaton, Reichenbacher, Sauter, Allen, Scott and Hill (2012, *Psychological Medicine*), **20 high-functioning autistic adults and 20 matched controls** categorising vocal and verbal emotional expressions: "Higher levels of alexithymia in the ASD group accounted for **some, but not all**, of the group difference in emotion recognition ability. However, alexithymia was insufficient to explain the different sensitivities of the two groups to the effects of psycho-acoustic complexity on performance." [Heaton2012 — VERIFIED]. This is a direct, well-designed partial disconfirmation from the same research group that supplied much of the supporting music evidence. The same paper found strong emotion-recognition/alexithymia associations *in controls*, which means alexithymia is a general-population dimension, not an autism-specific confound.

Keating, Kraaijkamp and Cook (2026) also report that even where group *levels* equalise after controlling alexithymia, the **mechanisms differ**: for non-autistic people, differentiating one's own emotions contributed to emotion recognition; for autistic people, precision of emotion *concepts* did [Keating2026a — VERIFIED]. Keating et al. (2026) separately show autistic and non-autistic facial expressions differ spatiotemporally even after controlling facial morphology *and* alexithymia, and that alexithymia predicted less differentiated angry and happy expressions — i.e. alexithymia explains some but not all of the production difference [Keating2026b — VERIFIED].

**Crucial warning for a music-based tool.** Alexithymia is not modality-blind. Suslow and Kersting (2021) reviewed 11 studies on alexithymia and emotion perception outside face/voice, and concluded that "alexithymia **might be associated with deficits in the perception of primarily negative but also positive emotions in music**" [Suslow2021 — VERIFIED]. Kinnaird et al. also note that studies controlling for both constructs found alexithymia rather than autism predicted difficulties in facial, vocal **and musical** emotion recognition [Kinnaird2019 — VERIFIED]. If ~50% of autistic users carry clinically significant alexithymia, and alexithymia degrades musical emotion perception too, then the sub-population with the greatest need for an externalisation aid is the sub-population least likely to read the output correctly. **This is the most serious scientific objection to the tool and should not be softened.**

**Interoception.** Contested, and recently trending toward null. Klein, Witthöft and Jungmann (2025) systematically reviewed **31 studies** and meta-analysed **5 adult studies** of cardiac interoceptive accuracy: **no significant difference between autistic adults and neurotypical controls (μ̂ = −.21, SE = .11, p = .06)**. Findings in children/adolescents and for interoceptive sensibility and awareness were "inconsistent — some studies reported reduced, increased, or similar interoceptive abilities"; the authors conclude "ASD is **not systematically associated with altered** cardiac interoceptive accuracy" [Klein2025 — VERIFIED]. Measurement itself is under attack: Adams et al. (2026, *Psychophysiology*) used a Phase Adjustment Task with 43 interoceptive participants and estimate that **53.5%–97.7% of delay-based interoceptive individuals perceive heartbeats at delays that do not correspond to the synchronous or asynchronous tone timings used in the standard two-alternative heartbeat detection task**, concluding it "should not be used to measure cardiac interoceptive accuracy" [Adams2026 — VERIFIED]. Practical consequence: **do not build any claim on "autistic people have poorer interoception"** — the evidence does not support it, and the dominant measure is probably invalid.

### 3. Auditory processing and sensory sensitivity — the hard constraints

**Prevalence.** Williams, Suzman and Woynaroski (2021, *Ear and Hearing* 42(5), 1137–1150) is the definitive estimate. 7,783 articles screened, 67 included; three-level Bayesian random-effects meta-analysis of interview/questionnaire measures (**k = 103, n_ASD = 13,093**):
- **Current hyperacusis prevalence: 41.42% (95% CrI 37.23–45.84%)**
- **Lifetime prevalence: 60.58% (95% CrI 50.37–69.76%)**
- Objective/observational measures (k = 8, n_ASD = 488): **27.30% (14.92–46.31%)**
- ADI-R-restricted sensitivity analysis gave similar values; prevalence **increased sharply with age** in ADI-R studies (BF₁₀ = 93.10, R²_Het = 0.692). Mean sample age across studies 7.88 years.
[Williams2021 — VERIFIED]

Williams et al. (in a preprint chapter using **936 autistic adults** from SPARK) state that "**between 50–70% of autistic individuals report clinically significant decreased sound tolerance (DST)** at some point in their lives", and — importantly — that DST is **not one thing**: hyperacusis (moderate-intensity sounds perceived as excessively loud or physically painful) and misophonia (specific trigger sounds causing anger, irritation, disgust) are distinct, strongly correlated conditions with partly different correlates. Both were independently associated with female sex, higher autistic traits, ADHD symptoms and phonophobia; hyperacusis specifically with hearing/neurological symptoms, misophonia specifically with anger symptoms [WilliamsDST — VERIFIED as preprint; prefer the peer-reviewed version when available].

Scheerer, Boucher, Arzanpour, Iarocci and Birmingham (2026, *Autism in Adulthood*) surveyed **77 autistic and 128 non-autistic adults who self-report DST**: clinically relevant misophonia and clinically relevant hyperacusis were both more prevalent among the autistic respondents; misophonia severity tracked autistic traits and anxiety, hyperacusis severity tracked autistic traits, anxiety, depression and **poorer quality of life** [Scheerer2026 — VERIFIED].

**DSM-5 status.** Sensory features were added in DSM-5 (2013) as one of the polythetic restricted/repetitive behaviour criteria; it "has remained understudied" [Russo2026 — VERIFIED]. Its diagnostic *specificity* is contested — one recent narrative review states the DSM-5 sensory criterion, "though neurobiologically grounded, **lacks diagnostic specificity**" [Croce2026 — VERIFIED, but note this is a narrative/argumentative review, not evidence].

**Structure of sensory reactivity.** Williams et al. (2023, *Molecular Autism*), integrative data analysis of **3,868 autistic youth across 12 data sources**, found that only **hyper**reactivity supports a valid supra-modal construct (ω_H = .800); hypo-reactivity (ω_H = .653) and seeking did not, and modality-specific scores add significant value [Williams2023 — VERIFIED]. Practical consequence: **auditory hyperreactivity must be profiled per-person and per-modality, not inferred from a global sensory score.**

**What autistic people say they need.** MacLennan, O'Brien and Tavassoli (2022, *JADD*), co-produced mixed-methods study with **49 autistic adults**, produced themes of "Outcomes", "**Control**", "Tolerance and management", and "The role of other people" [MacLennan2022 — VERIFIED]. Control is named as a first-order theme in the autistic account of sensory experience. I did **not** find a study in this session that enumerates the specific acoustic parameters (frequency, onset rate, dissonance) that autistic listeners rate as aversive — see Gaps. One psychophysical finding is suggestive rather than dispositive: Yu, Xie, Wang and Mottron (2026) found in **21 autistic and 23 TD children** that autistic children had significantly *higher* temporal gap-detection thresholds (worse temporal resolution) and significantly *lower* frequency-modulation thresholds (better spectral sensitivity) — an "Auditory Bias Index" favouring spectral over temporal cues [Yu2026 — VERIFIED]. Read as design input: **pitch/timbre carries information more reliably than fast temporal detail for at least some autistic listeners.**

### 4. Musical strengths and engagement

**Enhanced perceptual functioning.** Mottron, Dawson, Soulières, Hubert and Burack (2006, *JADD* 36, 27–43) proposed the EPF model: "locally oriented visual **and auditory** perception, enhanced low-level discrimination… autonomy of low-level information processing toward higher-order operations", and argued that "increased perceptual expertise may be implicated in the choice of special ability in savant autistics" [Mottron2006 — VERIFIED]. This is a theoretical model with accumulated support, not a single empirical finding.

**Pitch and absolute pitch.** Hisaizumi and Tantam (2024) narratively reviewed auditory perception and report "persuasive evidence" of differences, "supported by the additional finding of a **higher prevalence of absolute pitch and enhanced pitch discriminating abilities in autistic children** compared to neurotypical children", with enhanced pitch discrimination tending to occur in autistic individuals with a history of language delay [Hisaizumi2024 — VERIFIED — but this is a *narrative* review and gives no prevalence figure]. **I did not retrieve a numeric prevalence estimate for absolute pitch in autism, and one should not be asserted.** The measurement literature explains why: Bairnsfather, Mosing, Osborne and Wilson (2025) reviewed **160 studies (N = 23,221)** of absolute-pitch phenotyping and found that although 99% agree on the conceptual definition, classification thresholds ranged from **20% to 100% naming accuracy (mean 77%, SD 20)**, with wildly varying stimulus range and timbre — "limiting the generalisability of individual studies" [Bairnsfather2025 — VERIFIED]. Any "X% of autistic people have absolute pitch" figure is threshold-dependent and should be treated as unreliable.

Evidence on the autism-traits/AP link in the general population is genuinely mixed: Hsieh and Hsiao (2025, N = 120) report support for the link using a continuous AQ slider, with pitch-related skills the only cognitive predictor of autistic traits among AP musicians, while explicitly noting "evidence regarding the link between AP and autistic traits is currently inconclusive" [Hsieh2025 — VERIFIED].

**Rhythm and beat.** Dahary, Rimmer and Quintin (2024, *JADD*), **23 autistic and 23 neurotypical children aged 6–13** matched on chronological age and verbal/non-verbal mental age, beat-alignment detection: overall performance exceeded theoretical chance (50%) but **not statistical chance (70%) in either group**. On-beat accuracy was higher for the autistic group; the autistic group was equally accurate at detecting beat alignments but **less precise at detecting misalignments** [Dahary2024 — VERIFIED]. So beat perception is "spared" but not strong in either group — do not overclaim rhythmic sensitivity.

**A domain-specific strength with a control condition.** Heaton, Tsang, Jakubowski, Müllensiefen and Allen (2018) compared children with autism-plus-language-impairment (ALI), children with specific language impairment (SLI), and typically developing controls matched on age, receptive vocabulary, non-verbal IQ and digit span on a voluntary musical imagery task. The ALI group performed **as well as TD controls on tempo and better than TD controls on pitch**, despite auditory short-term memory and vocabulary impairments comparable to the SLI group, who performed significantly worse on both [Heaton2018 — VERIFIED]. This is a strong dissociation: the musical-imagery strength is not simply an artefact of general ability.

**Savant skills.** Park (2023) systematically reviewed 40 articles (2008–2022) on autism and savant syndrome and found cognitive accounts converging on weak central coherence, detail-focused processing, enhanced perceptual functioning and hyper-systemising, but concluded "**no single theory can specify** the cognitive characteristics" [Park2023 — VERIFIED]. **I did not obtain a verified prevalence figure for musical savant skills in autism** — see Gaps. Do not assert one.

**Engagement.** Reviews repeatedly assert that autistic children "show musical interests" and that music is preferred because it is "structured and systematic, and therefore more predictable than social interaction" [Jaschke2024, Dahary2024 — VERIFIED as statements in those papers, but these are authors' framing claims rather than measured prevalence of engagement]. Allen, Hill and Heaton (2009) provides the most direct qualitative evidence that autistic adults use music instrumentally across cognitive, emotional and social purposes — n = 12 [VERIFIED].

### 5. The double empathy problem

**The theory.** Milton (2012), "On the ontological status of autism: the 'double empathy problem'", *Disability & Society* 27(6), DOI 10.1080/09687599.2012.710008. The claim is that breakdowns between autistic and non-autistic people are a **bidirectional mismatch of perspectives and expectations**, not a unilateral deficit in the autistic party [Milton2012 — **UNVERIFIED** in this session; citation metadata confirmed from publisher and repository listings, content not retrieved].

**The strongest empirical test.** Crompton, Ropar, Evans-Williams, Flynn and Fletcher-Watson (2020, *Autism*), diffusion-chain design: **72 adults, 24 per group, 9 chains of 8 participants** (3 all-autistic, 3 all-non-autistic, 3 mixed alternating), sequentially retelling a 30-detail story.
- All-non-autistic chains M = 12.40 (SD 5.28); all-autistic chains M = 12.96 (SD 5.31); **p = 0.92** — no difference.
- **Mixed chains M = 8.92 (SD 4.34), significantly lower (p < .05)**; regression showed a steeper decline for mixed chains, F(5,66) = 77.05, R² = 0.85.
- Rapport was significantly lower for mixed chains; Bayesian ANOVA gave BF₁₀ = 62.32 distinguishing non-autistic from mixed, and BF₁₀ = 0.81 (negligible) between autistic and non-autistic.
- Stated limitations: modest sample; participants aware of diagnostic status; unequal gender distribution; sample restricted to typical-range-IQ, verbally fluent, adult-diagnosed people; weak Bayes factors for the autistic-vs-mixed rapport comparison warrant replication.
[Crompton2020 — VERIFIED, full PDF]

**Convergent work on the reverse direction.** Sheppard et al. (2026, *Autism*), **N = 128** non-autistic participants inferring what event a filmed person was reacting to: "Participants performed less well for videos of **autistic** than non-autistic others." Diagnostic labelling had little effect; autism knowledge, prior contact and stigma were **not** associated with relative ability. The authors conclude "it might be difficult to train people to read autistic people's non-verbal behaviour more effectively; instead, intervention might focus on raising awareness of this issue" [Sheppard2026 — VERIFIED]. Alhusayni, Sheppard, Mitchell and Marsh (2025), **n = 25** non-autistic students rating brief clips, found consistent negative bias in judging autistic students' academic motivation, success, grades and happiness [Alhusayni2025 — VERIFIED]. Keating et al. (2026) supply a mechanism: autistic and non-autistic expressions differ measurably in which facial landmarks carry anger, happiness and sadness — "autistic and non-autistic faces may be essentially 'speaking a different language'" [Keating2026b — VERIFIED].

**Contrary and qualifying evidence — report this.** Rum et al. (2026, *Molecular Autism*), empathic accuracy paradigm with **141 autistic and 94 non-autistic participants** (mean age 49.09) rating five autistic and five non-autistic storytellers: "**No significant main effects emerged for rater or target neurotype**" on continuous valence or discrete-emotion empathic accuracy. A trend-level interaction (p = .059, d = 0.13) hinted that autistic raters were relatively more accurate toward autistic targets. Non-autistic raters *self-reported* significantly higher empathy (p < .001, d = −0.67) and empathic interest (d = −0.43) regardless of target. The authors conclude "**partial support**" and warn that individual variability "cautions against treating neurotype" as a uniform predictor [Rum2026 — VERIFIED]. Qualitatively, autistic participants described difficulty identifying emotions and engaged in metacognitive introspection — consistent with alexithymia, and consistent with the finding that autistic people may *underestimate* their own empathic ability.

There is also active theoretical pushback: Reed and Vogus (2026) note "recent work has questioned this claim and the double empathy problem, generally," and propose that same-neurotype dyads may also misfire because neurodivergent people perform to neurotypical expectations ("performative relationality") [Reed2026 — VERIFIED as a conceptual paper, no data]. Crompton herself (2026, *BJPsych*) has written on the diagnostic implications [UNVERIFIED — abstract not available].

**What this implies for a translation tool.** Double empathy is a two-way claim, so a tool that renders one person's state into a form another person receives inherits the problem in both directions and must be evaluated in both: (a) can a non-autistic recipient read an autistic person's musical rendering accurately, and (b) can an autistic recipient read a non-autistic person's rendering? Crompton's result predicts that *same-neurotype* transfer will be fine and *cross-neurotype* transfer will degrade — and that the degradation will be attributed to the autistic party unless the tool is framed otherwise. Sheppard's result predicts that simply telling the recipient "this person is autistic" will **not** fix it.

### 6. Communication and externalisation aids generally

**AAC.** The evidence base is real but narrow. Mifsud, Thomas, Bowron and Sutherland (2026, *IJLCD*) systematically reviewed **69 papers** on AAC for autistic adults and children: most investigated speech-generating devices and low-tech picture exchange using behavioural techniques; **only three papers investigated outcomes with adults**; results were "largely descriptions of proximal outcomes, such as simple requests, with few considering generalisation of skills or distal outcomes such as quality of life"; "the quality of the papers was generally low". Their conclusion: "evidence regarding the impact of AAC on the broad range of communication functions and quality of life is **very limited**" [Mifsud2026 — VERIFIED]. Their title is the finding: "*The Predominant Focus Is Still on Teaching Children to Make Requests*." A tool for expressing **internal state** rather than **requests** is therefore addressing a genuine and documented gap in AAC — and has essentially no AAC evidence base to inherit. A narrative review of AAC in clinical/research settings found AAC aids "can reduce stress and anxiety, thereby improving adherence", but identified **no studies** on AAC and compliance in clinical research procedures [Passarini2026 — VERIFIED].

**Externalising internal states for reflection.** The closest well-evidenced mechanism is affect labelling. Lieberman, Eisenberger, Crockett, Tom, Pfeifer and Way (2007, *Psychological Science*) showed that affect labelling "diminished the response of the amygdala and other limbic regions to negative emotional images" and increased right ventrolateral prefrontal activity, with the two inversely correlated and mediated by medial PFC [Lieberman2007 — VERIFIED]. **But the affect-labelling literature has since become contested.** Ariely, Mokady, Reggev and Anholt (2026, *Affective Science*), two studies, **total N = 226**, replicated Nook et al. (2021): "affect labeling **reducing the effectiveness of reappraisal**", with no delayed benefit at 1–2 day follow-up; they conclude "affect labeling may **solidify** emerging emotions and limit emotional regulation flexibility" [Ariely2026 — VERIFIED]. So the premise that naming or externalising a feeling is straightforwardly beneficial does **not** hold unconditionally — and a tool that *fixes* a described state into a concrete musical artefact may share the solidification risk that verbal labelling has.

**Published work that renders emotional state as generated music.** The closest published system is **AffectMachine-Classical** (Agres, Dash and Chua, 2023, *Frontiers in Psychology* 14:1158172): a rule-based probabilistic real-time generator of affective classical music, "designed to be incorporated into biofeedback systems… to help users become aware of, and ultimately mediate, their own dynamic affective states." A listener validation study found the system "very effective in communicating various levels of **Arousal (R² = 0.96)**" and "quite convincing in terms of **Valence (R² = 0.90)**" [Agres2023 — VERIFIED]. Two caveats: the reported validation is that *listeners recover the intended affect*, not that *users benefit*; and the sample and population were not reported in the record I retrieved (no autistic-participant validation).

More broadly, Venkatesan, Demetriou, Hempel and Bowling (2026) scoping-reviewed **22 commercially available music-based digital therapeutics**, classifying them into preference-based selection, **affective parameterisation**, **affect matching and compensation**, neural entrainment, and biofeedback. Their conclusion is blunt: "while general evidence supporting musical-based interventions… is substantial, **evidence for MDTs specifically is presently too limited to draw conclusions about real world effectiveness**" [Venkatesan2026 — VERIFIED].

**On mood visualisation, emotion wheels and biofeedback displays specifically:** I found no autism-specific empirical literature in this session. See Gaps.

## What a music-based state-representation tool can claim scientifically, and the hard design constraints

1. **Claimable:** emotion perception in music is the modality where autistic–non-autistic differences are smallest, and in the only three-way within-participant comparison it was the modality where autistic children out-performed controls (ηp² = .11) [Sivathasan2023, Heaton1999, Quintin2011]. **Not claimable:** that musical emotion perception is uniformly intact — the only meta-analysis to include music found moderate deficits for musical *fear* (g = −0.50) and *sadness* (g = −0.63) on 2–4 datasets each [Leung2022]. State the claim as "smallest and least consistent difference", not "preserved".

2. **Strongest single warrant for the whole concept:** physiological responsiveness to music is intact in autistic adults while *verbal* report is reduced, and the verbal gap is fully mediated by alexithymia [Allen2013]. This is precisely the profile a non-verbal externalisation channel is designed to exploit. Cite this, not Heaton 1999, as the mechanism.

3. **The tool's largest scientific liability is alexithymia, not autism.** ~50% of autistic people meet TAS criteria versus ~5% of neurotypicals [Kinnaird2019], and alexithymia is associated with impaired perception of emotion **in music** as well as in faces and voices [Suslow2021, Kinnaird2019]. Design consequence: measure TAS-20 as a covariate in any evaluation, report outcomes stratified by alexithymia, and do not assume the musical output is legible to the users who most need it.

4. **Use arousal/energy as the primary axis and valence as secondary.** Autistic adults describe mood using internally focused arousal language more than externally focused emotive language [Allen2009]; the only validated affective music generator recovers arousal better than valence (R² = 0.96 vs 0.90) [Agres2023]; and both groups' arousal ratings were indistinguishable in the three-way comparison [Sivathasan2023]. Do not require the user to commit to a discrete emotion label to get a rendering.

5. **Prefer non-verbal response modes for any check that the rendering is right.** Task demand is a significant moderator: verbal-response emotion tasks produce group differences across all emotions, non-verbal tasks only for disgust [Leung2022]. A "does this sound like how you feel?" interaction should offer sliders, comparison pairs or selection, not free-text.

6. **Loudness is a safety constraint, not a preference.** Current hyperacusis prevalence in autism is 41.4% (95% CrI 37.2–45.8%), lifetime 60.6% (n = 13,093) [Williams2021]; 50–70% report clinically significant decreased sound tolerance at some point [WilliamsDST]. Ship a conservative default output level well below typical media loudness, hard-limit peak and integrated loudness, expose calibration before any first playback, and never normalise upward.

7. **Hyperacusis and misophonia are different failure modes and need different mitigations** [WilliamsDST, Scheerer2026]. Hyperacusis argues for level, spectral-tilt and dynamic-range control; misophonia argues for *timbre and sample choice* control — specific sounds trigger anger and disgust regardless of level. Offer instrument/timbre exclusion lists, and avoid mouth, breath, chewing-adjacent and repetitive percussive-transient sounds by default.

8. **No sudden onsets, no unsignalled starts.** Autistic children in one psychophysical study showed *worse* temporal gap detection and *better* spectral sensitivity than controls [Yu2026], and sensory hyperreactivity is the one supra-modal sensory construct with solid psychometric support [Williams2023]. Fade in; never auto-play; make the first sound predictable and preceded by a visible countdown or explicit user action.

9. **Control is a first-order requirement named by autistic adults themselves** [MacLennan2022]. A large, always-visible, single-action stop; volume reachable without navigating; pause/resume; no modal dialogue between the user and silence; and no rendering triggered by anything other than an explicit user action. Treat "stop" latency as a correctness property.

10. **Keep renderings short and terminating, and let the user re-trigger.** There is no evidence base on safe exposure duration for this population; the conservative default is a bounded clip the user can repeat, not a stream. Also avoid unresolved dissonance held over time and avoid escalating intensity contours — but note honestly that **I found no direct evidence on autistic aversion to dissonance specifically**, so treat this as prudence, not science.

11. **Profile sensitivity per person and per modality; do not infer it.** Supra-modal hypo-reactivity and seeking constructs are psychometrically inadequate, and modality-specific scores add significant value [Williams2023]. Onboarding should ask about *sound* directly, and re-ask, since hyperacusis prevalence rises with age [Williams2021].

12. **For the receiving direction, assume cross-neurotype degradation and design against attributing it to the autistic user.** Mixed-neurotype chains lost ~30% more story detail than same-neurotype chains (8.92 vs 12.40/12.96 details, p < .05) [Crompton2020]; non-autistic observers read autistic people less accurately, and telling them the diagnosis does not help [Sheppard2026]. Evaluate recipient accuracy in both directions and in same- and mixed-neurotype pairs. Present any mismatch as a two-way translation gap, not a failure of the sender. Note the contrary evidence: one empathic-accuracy study with 235 participants found no neurotype main effects at all [Rum2026].

13. **Do not build on interoception.** The claim that autistic people have poorer interoception is not supported meta-analytically (cardiac interoceptive accuracy μ̂ = −.21, p = .06, k = 5) [Klein2025], and the standard measure is probably invalid [Adams2026]. Any framing of the tool as "compensating for poor interoception" is unsupported and will not survive review.

14. **Do not claim therapeutic benefit.** Music-intervention meta-analyses in autism are mostly non-significant or low-quality [Navarro2025, Applewhite2022], transdiagnostic effects do not survive follow-up and are low/very-low GRADE [Lassner2024], and commercial music-based digital therapeutics have "too limited" evidence for real-world effectiveness [Venkatesan2026]. The defensible claim is **communication and reflection aid**, not intervention. Relatedly, the affect-labelling premise is contested — labelling can *impair* subsequent reappraisal (N = 226 replication) [Ariely2026] — so a rendering that reifies a state may entrench it; build in revision and re-rendering rather than a single authoritative output.

## Gaps

- **No verified acoustic-parameter evidence on aversiveness.** I found no study enumerating which frequencies, onset rates, degrees of dissonance or dynamic ranges autistic listeners rate as aversive. Constraints 8 and 10 above are prudential inference from hyperacusis/misophonia prevalence and from Yu2026's spectral/temporal bias, not direct evidence. This is the highest-value gap to close before implementation.
- **No verified prevalence figure for absolute pitch in autism**, and the AP phenotyping literature shows thresholds ranging 20–100% across 160 studies, making any such figure unreliable [Bairnsfather2025]. Likewise **no verified prevalence figure for musical savant skills** in autism.
- **The music arm of the emotion-recognition meta-analysis rests on 2–4 datasets per emotion** [Leung2022]. The claim that musical emotion recognition is preserved in autism is supported by a handful of small studies (n = 14, 26, 25 per group) and one meta-analysis that partly contradicts them. This literature is small and contested and should be described that way.
- **No study, as far as this search reached, renders a person's *described* (i.e. self-reported, verbally specified) psychological state as music for them to reflect on.** AffectMachine-Classical renders a *target affect* or a *biofeedback signal* [Agres2023]; music-based digital therapeutics do affect matching/compensation from selection or physiology [Venkatesan2026]. The description-to-music-for-reflection loop appears to be novel — which also means there is no efficacy evidence for it.
- **No autism-specific empirical literature retrieved on mood visualisation, emotion wheels, or biofeedback displays.** This may exist; my searches did not surface it.
- **No validation of any affective music generator with autistic participants.**
- **Crompton 2020's sample is verbally fluent, typical-IQ, adult-diagnosed adults** — as are most of the emotion-recognition samples above (Heaton 1999 IQ-matched children; Quintin 2011 "high-functioning"; Allen 2009 and 2013 "high-functioning"). **Nothing in this evidence base speaks to autistic people with intellectual disability or minimal speech**, who are arguably the population an externalisation aid would most help.
- **Milton 2012 content not retrieved**; cited on metadata only.
- **Leung et al.'s journal of record needs confirmation** (Developmental Review vs Research in Autism Spectrum Disorders).
- **TIME-A** and other large music-therapy RCTs were not retrieved; no figures attributed.
