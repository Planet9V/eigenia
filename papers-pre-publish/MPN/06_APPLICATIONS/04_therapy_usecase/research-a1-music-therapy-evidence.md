# a1 — State of the evidence for music therapy: autism specifically, and related applications generally

Prepared 2026-09-12. Every numeric claim below was read off a page I fetched, and is marked VERIFIED in Sources. Where I could not open a source, the claim is marked UNVERIFIED and no numbers are given for it.

## Findings

### 1. The TIME-A trial (the largest RCT in the field, and negative) [TIMEA-JAMA] VERIFIED

Bieleninik Ł, Geretsegger M, Mössler K, et al. "Effects of Improvisational Music Therapy vs Enhanced Standard Care on Symptom Severity Among Children With Autism Spectrum Disorder: The TIME-A Randomized Clinical Trial." *JAMA* 2017;318(6):525–535. DOI 10.1001/jama.2017.9478. ISRCTN78923965.

**Design.** Assessor-blinded RCT at 10 centres across 9 countries (Australia, Austria, Brazil, Israel, Italy, Korea, Norway, UK, USA). n = 364 randomised, 1:1 (182 improvisational music therapy [IMT] + enhanced standard care; 182 enhanced standard care alone). Children aged 4–7 years, mean age 5.4, 83% boys. Retention: 314/364 (86%) at the 5-month endpoint; 290/364 (80%) at 12 months.

**Intervention.** 30-minute one-to-one improvisational music therapy sessions over 5 months, delivered at one of two pre-assigned intensities: high (3×/week, n = 90) or low (1×/week, n = 92). **Median 19 sessions actually delivered.**

**Comparator.** Enhanced standard care = 3 sessions of parent counselling plus whatever local usual care the family was already receiving. The control arm received a **median of 45 other therapy sessions** over the same 5 months — so this was an active, well-served comparator, not a do-nothing control.

**Primary outcome.** ADOS social affect domain (range 0–27, higher = more severe) at 5 months.
- IMT: 14.08 at baseline → 13.23 at 5 months.
- ESC: 13.49 at baseline → 12.58 at 5 months.
- **Between-group difference 0.06 (95% CI −0.70 to 0.81; P = .88). Null.**
- At 12 months: IMT 12.60, ESC 11.72; no significant between-group difference.

**Secondary outcomes.** 20 exploratory secondary outcomes; **17 showed no significant between-group difference.** ADOS social affect at 2 and 12 months: null. Parent-rated Social Responsiveness Scale total at 2, 5 and 12 months: null. Nominally significant, unblinded, exploratory signals appeared on a few SRS subscales (social motivation at 5 months; autistic mannerisms at 2 and 12 months) and on a quality-of-life visual analogue scale for the high-intensity arm at 5 months. These are parent-report, unblinded, and unadjusted for multiplicity.

**Post hoc responder analysis** (explicitly post hoc, so hypothesis-generating only): ≥1-point ADOS social affect improvement at 5 months in 95/182 (52%) IMT vs 76/182 (42%) ESC, RR 1.25 (95% CI 1.00–1.56; P = .047); among those receiving ≥15 sessions, 58% vs 42%, RR 1.39 (95% CI 1.11–1.74; P = .004).

**Harms.** No serious adverse events and no other adverse events reported; institutional-stay rates stable or decreasing in both arms.

**Authors' stated conclusion (verbatim).** "Among children with autism spectrum disorder, improvisational music therapy, compared with enhanced standard care, resulted in no significant difference in symptom severity based on the ADOS social affect domain over 5 months. **These findings do not support the use of improvisational music therapy for symptom reduction in children with autism spectrum disorder.**"

**Note on the UK/HTA arm** [TIMEA-HTA] VERIFIED: the NIHR HTA report of the same trial gives the same primary result (mean change in social affect at 5 months, difference 0.06, 95% CI −0.70 to 0.81) and concludes "Adding IMT to other treatments received by children aged 4–7 years with ASD does not appear to improve the core symptoms of this disorder." (The HTA report lists 8 countries for its own recruitment framing; the JAMA paper reports 10 centres in 9 countries. Use the JAMA figures.)

### 2. Cochrane: 2014 vs 2022 — the conclusions moved, and not in the direction usually cited

**2014 — Geretsegger M, Elefant C, Mössler KA, Gold C. "Music therapy for people with autism spectrum disorder." CDSR 2014, CD004381.pub3.** [COCH-2014] VERIFIED
- 10 RCTs, **165 participants** (children).
- Reported SMDs favouring music therapy: social interaction within the therapy context 1.06 (95% CI 0.02 to 2.10); generalised social interaction outside therapy 0.71 (0.18 to 1.25), moderate quality; non-verbal communication within therapy 0.57 (0.29 to 0.85); verbal communication 0.33 (0.16 to 0.49); initiating behaviour 0.73 (0.36 to 1.11), moderate quality; social-emotional reciprocity 2.28 (0.73 to 3.83), low quality. Secondary: social adaptation 0.41, joy 0.96, parent–child relationship quality 0.82.
- Conclusion: "Music therapy may help children with ASD to improve skills in social interaction, verbal communication, initiating behaviour, and social-emotional reciprocity," explicitly limited by "small sample sizes of the studies."

**2022 — Geretsegger M, et al. "Music therapy for autistic people." CDSR 2022, CD004381.pub4, published 9 May 2022.** [COCH-2022] VERIFIED
- 26 studies, **1,165 participants** (16 new studies added). Intervention duration 3 days to 8 months; individual and group settings; 21 studies in children aged 2–12, 5 in older children/young adults; over half of studies from North America or Asia.
- Effects immediately post-intervention, with GRADE certainty:
  - Global improvement: **RR 1.22 (95% CI 1.06 to 1.40)**, 8 studies / 583 participants — **moderate** certainty. NNTB 11 (low-risk populations) to 6 (high-risk).
  - Quality of life: **SMD 0.28 (0.06 to 0.49)**, 3 RCTs / 340 participants — **moderate**.
  - Total autism symptom severity: **SMD −0.83 (−1.41 to −0.24)**, 9 studies / 575 participants — **moderate**.
  - Social interaction: **SMD 0.26 (−0.05 to 0.57)** — **low**. Crosses null.
  - Non-verbal communication: **SMD 0.26 (−0.03 to 0.55)** — **low**. Crosses null.
  - Verbal communication: **SMD 0.30 (−0.18 to 0.78)** — **very low**. Crosses null.
  - Adverse events: **RR 1.52 (0.39 to 5.94)** — **moderate**; i.e. probably no increase in harm.
- Conclusion (verbatim): "The findings of this updated review provide evidence that music therapy is probably associated with an increased chance of global improvement for autistic people, likely helps them to improve total autism severity and quality of life, and probably does not increase adverse events immediately after the intervention."

**How 2022 differs from 2014 — stated plainly.**
- **The 2014 headline claims did not survive.** In 2014 the review's positive claims were precisely about *social interaction, verbal communication, initiating behaviour and social-emotional reciprocity*, with large SMDs. In 2022, with 7× the participants, **social interaction, non-verbal communication and verbal communication all have confidence intervals that cross the null**, at low to very low certainty. The communication/interaction claim that is most often quoted from "the Cochrane review on music therapy and autism" is the 2014 claim, and it has been superseded.
- **The surviving claims are different and broader-grained**: global improvement (a clinician-rated global impression construct), total symptom severity, and quality of life — at moderate certainty.
- **The certainty language changed**: 2022 uses GRADE wording ("probably", "likely") rather than 2014's "may help ... limited by small sample sizes".
- **Internal tension the review does not resolve**: the moderate-certainty severity estimate (SMD −0.83, 9 studies, 575 participants) sits in the same review as TIME-A, a single 364-participant trial that was null on a blinded observational severity measure. The pooled severity estimate rests on fewer total participants than the headline review size and has a wide CI (−1.41 to −0.24). Anyone citing −0.83 without also citing TIME-A's 0.06 (−0.70 to 0.81) is presenting half the evidence.

### 3. Other systematic reviews and meta-analyses since 2017

**Gassner L, Geretsegger M, Mayer-Ferbas J (2022), "Effectiveness of music therapy for autism spectrum disorder, dementia, depression, insomnia and schizophrenia: update of systematic reviews," *Eur J Public Health* 32(1):27–34, DOI 10.1093/eurpub/ckab042.** [GASSNER-2022] VERIFIED
- Update of five Cochrane reviews; literature search 2013–2020, four databases; **10 RCTs, 1,248 patients**, moderate-to-low risk of bias.
- Autism: two update trials (n = 415) found improvement in family quality of life and social communication skills, **but symptom severity showed no differential improvement**. Narrative claims of improved "behaviour, social communication, brain connectivity and parent–child relationship."
- Insomnia: three update trials (n = 249) — **receptive music listening** improved subjective sleep quality and psychological QoL; **objective sleep parameters did not improve**.
- Dementia: four update trials (n = 554) — improvements in mood, neuropsychiatric behaviour, apathy, communication, physical function; cognition improved with **active methods only**.
- Depression: one update trial (n = 30). Schizophrenia: no new qualifying studies.

**Bu X, et al. (2025), "The effectiveness of music therapy in improving behavioral symptoms among children with autism spectrum disorders: a systematic review and meta-analysis," *Front Psychiatry* 15:1511920, DOI 10.3389/fpsyt.2024.1511920.** [BU-2025] VERIFIED
- 13 RCTs, **1,160 participants**, ages 1–14, searched to 18 Sept 2024.
- Pooled **SMD −0.66 (95% CI −0.93 to −0.39, p < 0.001)** on behavioural symptoms.
- **I² = 78%** — high heterogeneity, attributed by the authors to varying music styles, duration, assessment tools and therapist selection.
- **No GRADE applied.** Study quality rated "moderate" on the Cochrane risk-of-bias tool; the authors note blinding is inherently infeasible.
- **Disagrees with TIME-A, and says so**: the authors acknowledge Bieleninik et al. found no difference on ADOS and attribute the discrepancy to "measurement tool sensitivity and methodology differences." They also acknowledge that **the majority of included studies were conducted in China**, only five databases were searched, individual samples were small, and adverse-event reporting was incomplete. This is the standard shape of the disagreement: small, unblinded, often single-centre trials with subjective/parent-rated or locally developed outcomes produce moderate-to-large pooled effects; the one large multi-site trial with a blinded standardised observational primary outcome produced nothing.

**Marquez-Garcia AV, Magnuson J, Morris J, Iarocci G, Doesburg S, Moreno S (2022), "Music Therapy in Autism Spectrum Disorder: a Systematic Review," *Review Journal of Autism and Developmental Disorders* 9:91–107, DOI 10.1007/s40489-021-00246-x.** [MARQUEZ-2022] VERIFIED
- Scoped explicitly to "two methods of music therapy: improvisational music therapy (IMT) and singing/listening to songs"; also mentions rhythm-based activity, Orff-based music therapy, and auditory-motor mapping.
- Conclusion (verbatim): "The methodological limitations found in the current literature prevent us from making a strong statement about the effects of music therapy in autism."

### 4. Guidelines

[NCAEP-2021] VERIFIED

This is the strongest formal US classification statement in favour of music-based intervention in autism, and it is a practice-classification exercise, not a GRADE-rated effectiveness review, and its literature window closes at 2017.

### 5. The profession: who may deliver music therapy

**United Kingdom — statutory, protected title.** [HCPC] VERIFIED. The Health and Care Professions Council regulates arts therapists. The protected titles include **"Art psychotherapist", "Art therapist", "Dramatherapist" and "Music therapist"**. "To use one of these titles, an individual must be registered with the HCPC." Misuse of title is investigated as a regulatory matter. So in the UK, *calling* a product or a person's activity "music therapy" delivered by "a music therapist" has legal weight.

**United States — voluntary board certification plus patchwork state licensure.** [AMTA-SCOPE, CBMT] VERIFIED.
- AMTA's Scope of Music Therapy Practice defines music therapy as "the clinical and evidence-based use of music interventions to accomplish individualized goals for people of all ages and ability levels **within a therapeutic relationship by a credentialed professional** who has completed an approved music therapy program."
- Qualification: a bachelor's degree or higher from an AMTA-approved music therapy programme, **a minimum of 1,200 hours of supervised clinical work**, and the **MT-BC** credential from the Certification Board for Music Therapists.
- CBMT's stated mission: "CBMT promotes excellence in music therapy by awarding board certification based on proven, up-to-date knowledge and competence in clinical practice," with a vision "to ensure access to safe and competent music therapy practice." (CBMT's homepage points to separate pages for eligibility, examination, recertification and state licensure requirements; I did not read those, so I give no recertification cycle figures.)
- **Scope-of-practice boundary, verbatim in substance**: the AMTA document warns about "individuals attempting to provide music therapy treatment interventions without formalized music therapy training and credentials", and distinguishes the credentialed therapist's ability to "independently analyze client non-verbal, verbal, psychological, and physiological responses to music" from untrained use. It also allows explicitly that **"other professionals may use music, as appropriate, as long as they are working within their scope."**
- The operative distinction for a software tool: *using music* is unregulated; *practising music therapy* and *using the title* are not. A generated-music system is a tool that may be used by a therapist within their scope; it is not itself music therapy, and neither AMTA nor HCPC language supports it being described as such.

**Europe.** [EMTC] VERIFIED (partial). The European Music Therapy Confederation describes itself as supporting "European music therapy country associations to empower music therapists as they serve their clients", and maintains pages for training programmes, recommended training standards and CPD. **I could not read the registration pages, so the European Music Therapy Register (EMTR) criteria are UNVERIFIED here.**

**No scope-of-practice document I read addresses AI- or algorithmically generated music as a tool within music therapy.** That is a genuine silence, not a permission.

### 6. Where music-based intervention evidence IS strong — the comparators

**Rhythmic auditory cueing in Parkinsonian gait.** Ghai S, Ghai I, Schmitz G, Effenberg AO (2018), "Effect of rhythmic auditory cueing on parkinsonian gait: A systematic review and meta-analysis," *Scientific Reports* 8:506, DOI 10.1038/s41598-017-16232-5. [GHAI-2018] VERIFIED
- 50 studies (7 RCTs, 43 controlled clinical trials), **1,892 participants**; mean PEDro score **5.4/10 (fair)**.
- Gait velocity, overall pooled: **Hedges' g 0.23 (95% CI 0.10–0.30), I² = 87.4%**. Note this headline effect is *small* and extremely heterogeneous.
- Subgroups (heterogeneity much lower, effects larger): "off" medication g 0.43 (0.11–0.75, I² 18.8%); "on" medication g 0.55 (0.23–0.87, I² 0%); treadmill training g 1.0 (0.33–1.67); **fast-paced cueing g 0.70 (0.50–0.89, I² 0%)**; **slow-paced cueing g −0.24 (−0.51–0.19)** — i.e. the *direction of effect depends on the tempo relative to the patient's own cadence.*
- Overall: positive effect on velocity and stride length, negative on cadence.

**Stroke and acquired brain injury.** Magee WL, Clark I, Tamplin J, Bradt J (2017), "Music interventions for acquired brain injury," *Cochrane Database Syst Rev*, CD006787.pub3, published 19 Jan 2017. [MAGEE-2017] VERIFIED
- 29 RCTs, **775 adult participants**.
- Gait velocity **+11.34 m/min (95% CI 8.40–14.28)**, 9 trials / 268 participants — **moderate** quality. This is the single best-supported music-based effect in the whole literature reviewed here.
- Stride length **+0.12 m (0.04–0.20)**, 5 trials / 129 — **moderate**.
- Gait cadence **+10.77 steps/min (4.36–17.18)**, 7 trials / 223 — **low**.
- Upper-extremity timing **−1.08 sec (−1.69 to −0.47)**, 2 trials / 122 — **very low**.
- Communication overall **SMD 0.75 (0.11–1.39)**, 3 trials / 67 — **very low**. Naming **+9.79 (1.37–18.21)**, 2 trials / 35 — **very low**. Speech repetition **+8.90 (3.25–14.55)**, 2 trials / 35 — **very low**.
- Quality of life **SMD 0.89 (0.32–1.46)**, 2 trials / 53 — **low**.
- The review notes that **"a strong beat within music may be more effective than"** interventions without it, and that RAS may be beneficial for post-stroke gait parameters. Authors still conclude "more high-quality randomised controlled trials are needed."

**Melodic intonation therapy in aphasia.** Popescu T, Stahl B, Wiernik BM, Haiduk F, Zemanek M, Helm H, Matzinger T, Beisteiner R, Fitch WT (2022), "Melodic Intonation Therapy for aphasia: A multi-level meta-analysis of randomised controlled trials and individual participant data," medRxiv preprint 10.1101/2021.08.28.21262764. [POPESCU-2022] VERIFIED (as a preprint; peer-reviewed status not confirmed on the page I read)
- 22 eligible studies; **129 treated participants (59 in RCTs, 70 IPD) and 62 controls**. This is a *tiny* literature.
- On validated measures in RCT data: non-communicative language expression showed a small-to-moderate effect with "substantial uncertainty"; **communication ability: negligible effects**; language comprehension: weak effects with wide CIs.
- Critical methodological finding: **MIT's effect size was 5.7× larger in non-RCT than in RCT data**, which the authors attribute to confounding by spontaneous recovery; unvalidated measures substantially inflated effects relative to validated tests.
- Conclusion: MIT has a promising role in *trained repetition tasks* but "possible limitations in promoting everyday communication ability."
- Note: this is the same pattern as autism — large effects in weak designs, shrinking toward null as design quality and outcome validity rise.

**Music and dementia.** van der Steen JT, et al., "Music-based therapeutic interventions for people with dementia," *Cochrane Database Syst Rev*, CD003477.pub5, published 7 March 2025. [COCH-DEM-2025] VERIFIED
- 30 studies across 15 countries, **1,720 randomised (1,366 in meta-analyses)**; most participants institutionalised; 10 studies gave long-term data.
- Versus usual care, at end of treatment:
  - Depressive symptoms **SMD −0.23 (95% CI −0.42 to −0.04)** — **moderate**.
  - Overall behavioural problems **SMD −0.31 (−0.60 to −0.02)** — **low**.
  - **Agitation/aggression SMD −0.05 (−0.27 to 0.17) — moderate certainty. NULL.**
  - Emotional well-being **SMD 0.14 (−0.29 to 0.56)** — low to very low. Anxiety **−0.15 (−0.39 to 0.09)** — low to very low. Cognition **0.19 (−0.02 to 0.41)** — low to very low.
- Versus other activities: social behaviour **SMD 0.52 (0.08–0.96)** — low; anxiety **−0.75 (−1.27 to −0.24)** — very low.
- **"There was no evidence of long-term effects"** at four or more weeks post-treatment.
- **This matters and is frequently misreported.** "Music helps agitation in dementia" is the single most commonly cited music-therapy claim in the wild, and the most recent Cochrane update finds a moderate-certainty null on agitation/aggression. The surviving claim is a small effect on depressive symptoms.

### 7. Receptive vs improvisational vs compositional — and whether *generated* music is covered

**What the autism evidence base actually studies.**
- TIME-A — the largest and most rigorous trial — tested **live one-to-one improvisational music therapy** with a trained therapist, delivered under a manualised improvisational approach. [TIMEA-JAMA] VERIFIED
- The most recent scoping systematic review of the autism literature (Marquez-Garcia 2022) frames the field as covering **two methods: improvisational music therapy, and singing/listening to songs**, with rhythm-based, Orff-based and auditory-motor-mapping approaches also present. [MARQUEZ-2022] VERIFIED
- Cochrane 2022's outcome set — social interaction, verbal and non-verbal communication, social-emotional reciprocity, parent–child relationship quality — is a **relational** outcome set. These are constructs that presuppose an interaction partner. [COCH-2022, COCH-2014] VERIFIED
- **I found no systematic review, meta-analysis or RCT in autism that isolates pre-composed or machine-generated music used as a therapeutic object, and no subgroup analysis of it in any of the reviews I read.** Stated plainly: **the autism evidence base is, to the extent I could verify it, about live relational music-making between a therapist and a client, plus some song-based/listening work delivered by a person. A generated-score system cannot claim it.**

**Where non-live, pre-recorded or machine-produced audio *does* have evidence — and what mechanism it is.**
- **Rhythmic auditory cueing** (Ghai 2018; Magee 2017) frequently uses metronome or pre-recorded rhythmic audio, and Magee's Cochrane explicitly notes that a strong beat within music may be more effective than interventions without one. This is the **one** place in this literature where a non-live, reproducible audio stimulus carries moderate-certainty evidence. [GHAI-2018, MAGEE-2017] VERIFIED
- **But the mechanism is sensorimotor entrainment** — a periodic stimulus that a motor system locks onto — and Ghai's subgroup results show the effect **reverses sign with tempo** (fast-paced cueing g 0.70; slow-paced g −0.24). It is a timing prosthesis, not an emotional or expressive one. It provides no support at all for the proposition that music rendering a described psychological state produces therapeutic benefit.
- **Receptive music listening** has evidence for **subjective sleep quality** in insomnia (3 trials, n = 249) but **not for objective sleep parameters** (Gassner 2022). [GASSNER-2022] VERIFIED. That is recorded music, but it is also not autism and not symptom-modifying.
- AMTA's own scope document draws the line: music therapy requires a credentialed professional working "within a therapeutic relationship"; other professionals may use music within their own scope. The relationship, not the audio, is the defined therapeutic agent. [AMTA-SCOPE] VERIFIED

**Evidence for AI- or algorithmically generated music as a health intervention (any population).**
- Wei Q, He W (2026), "The application of AI-assisted music therapy tools in mental health interventions," *Front Psychol*, DOI 10.3389/fpsyg.2026.1741463 — a **narrative review**. It states "the evidence base remains uneven, and many studies rely on preliminary or small-sample evaluations; more rigorous designs (e.g., randomized controlled trials and external validation) are needed" and that "controlled evidence remains heterogeneous and often limited by small samples, inconsistent endpoints, and short follow-up," positioning AI-assisted music therapy as "a promising, rapidly developing adjunct rather than a replacement for established clinical care." **It identifies no robust body of RCTs and presents no AI-specific evidence for autism.** [WEI-2026] VERIFIED
- Khanh NT, Nguyen H-T, Pham Nhat VD, Chan CWH (2026), "Can AI-Generated Music Interventions Improve Health Outcomes? A Systematic Review," 37th International Nursing Research Congress (conference presentation). Eight databases searched to Sept 2025; **8 studies, 1,267 participants (4 RCTs, 4 quasi-experimental)**; outcomes in anxiety, sleep, cognition and physiological relaxation markers; **quality rated moderate in half the studies and weak in half**. **No autism findings reported.** This is a conference abstract, not a peer-reviewed journal article, and should be weighted accordingly. [KHANH-2026] VERIFIED
- There exist conference-paper-level descriptions of algorithmic-composition systems for ASD (e.g. a Springer chapter on a "Personalized Music Therapy Information System Based on Algorithmic Composition in the Treatment of Autism Spectrum Disorder"), which I did not read and which are engineering/system papers rather than controlled trials. **UNVERIFIED.**

## What a paper proposing a generated-music tool may and may not claim from this evidence

- **May not** claim that music therapy is an evidence-based treatment for core autism symptoms. The largest trial (n = 364, 10 centres, blinded primary outcome) is flatly null — difference 0.06, 95% CI −0.70 to 0.81 — and its authors wrote that the findings "do not support the use of improvisational music therapy for symptom reduction."
- **May not** cite the 2014 Cochrane review's social-interaction and communication effect sizes as current. They were superseded in 2022: social interaction 0.26 (−0.05 to 0.57, low), non-verbal 0.26 (−0.03 to 0.55, low), verbal 0.30 (−0.18 to 0.78, very low). All cross the null.
- **May** state accurately that the 2022 Cochrane review found moderate-certainty benefit for global improvement (RR 1.22, 1.06–1.40), quality of life (SMD 0.28, 0.06–0.49) and total symptom severity (SMD −0.83, −1.41 to −0.24) — but only if it cites TIME-A's null in the same breath, and notes that the severity pool is 575 participants across 9 studies.
- **May not** transfer *any* of that to a generated score. Every one of those estimates comes from interventions delivered by a human therapist; none isolates pre-composed or machine-generated music; no review reports a subgroup for it.
- **May not** claim a therapeutic relationship. AMTA's definition requires "a therapeutic relationship by a credentialed professional"; Cochrane's autism outcomes are relational constructs. A system that emits audio is not a party to a relationship.
- **May not** use the term "music therapy" for the system, or describe it as delivering music therapy, particularly in a UK context where "Music therapist" is an HCPC-protected title. The defensible framing is: a tool that a credentialed music therapist might use within their existing scope — and AMTA explicitly permits other professionals to "use music" within their own scope.
- **May** point to rhythmic auditory stimulation as proof that a *reproducible, non-live* audio stimulus can carry moderate-certainty clinical benefit — gait velocity +11.34 m/min (8.40–14.28) after stroke. **May not** extend that to expressive or emotional content: the RAS mechanism is sensorimotor entrainment, and its effect reverses sign with tempo (g 0.70 fast-paced vs −0.24 slow-paced).
- **May not** cite "music helps agitation in dementia." The 2025 Cochrane update reports agitation/aggression SMD −0.05 (−0.27 to 0.17) at **moderate** certainty — a well-characterised null — with no evidence of long-term effects at ≥4 weeks.
- **May not** cite melodic intonation therapy as a strong precedent. The best meta-analysis found 129 treated participants total, negligible effects on communication ability, and effects 5.7× larger in non-RCT than RCT data.
- **May** state that AI-generated music as a health intervention has, at present, 8 studies / 1,267 participants (4 RCTs) across anxiety, sleep and cognition, with half rated weak quality and **none in autism** — and that this is the honest size of the direct evidence base. The claim floor is "untested in this population," not "promising."
- **May not** claim safety by inheritance. Cochrane 2022's adverse-event estimate (RR 1.52, 0.39–5.94) concerns therapist-delivered music therapy. A generated-audio system in autistic users raises distinct, unstudied risks (sensory sensitivity, aversive or unpredictable stimuli, misattribution of an emotional state back to the user) that no trial in this literature examined.
- **Should** state that no autism guideline recommends music therapy for core features: NICE CG170 does not address it at all. The strongest formal US classification is NCAEP's inclusion of "music-mediated intervention" among 28 EBPs on a literature window closing in 2017 — a practice-classification, not a GRADE effectiveness rating.

The conclusion that the base is "overwhelmingly live relational" rests on TIME-A, the 2014 review, Marquez-Garcia's framing and the relational outcome set — strong circumstantially, but a direct table of methods would settle it and should be extracted from CD004381.pub4 directly.

7. **Sharda et al. (2018, Translational Psychiatry) RCT** of music intervention in autism is frequently cited alongside TIME-A and was not verified here; it should be read directly, since it is the main single-trial counterweight cited against TIME-A.
8. **No health-economic or harms literature on generated-music systems in autistic users** was found at all.
9. **Publication-language bias**: Bu 2025 acknowledges most included trials were conducted in China and only five databases were searched. A language/region-stratified sensitivity analysis of the autism meta-analyses does not appear to exist and would materially change how the pooled effects should be read.
