**What Lacan's reading actually is.** The YFS text is the primary evidence; I read the full extracted
text. The reading has five load-bearing claims, and none of them is "Hamlet has an Oedipus complex."

*(a) The play is structured by the desire of the Other, concretely the mother's desire.* Lacan sets
this up at the start of the Ophelia lesson:

> "Our first step in this direction was to express the extent to which the play is dominated by the
> Mother as Other [*Autre*], i.e., the primordial subject of the demand [*la demande*]."
> (p. 12) [LACAN-YFS]

> "How is the desire of the Other manifested in the very perspective of this subject, Prince Hamlet?
> This desire, of the mother, is essentially manifested in the fact that, confronted on one hand with
> an eminent, idealized, exalted object — his father — and on the other with the degraded,
> despicable object Claudius, the criminal and adulterous brother, Hamlet does not choose."
> (p. 12) [LACAN-YFS]

The scandal for Hamlet is not that Gertrude sinned but that her enjoyment is *unproblematic*: the
"sacrosanct genital object … appears to her as an object to be enjoyed [*objet d'une jouissance*] in
what is truly the direct satisfaction of a need, and nothing else" (p. 12). That is what makes Hamlet
"waver in his abjuration of his mother" [LACAN-YFS].

*(b) "The hour of the Other" — Hamlet's time is never his own.* Lacan states it as a structural
thesis about neurosis before applying it:

> "This is at the base of neurotic behavior, in its most general form: the subject tries to find his
> sense of time [*lire son heure*] in his object, and it is even in the object that he will learn to
> tell time [*lire l'heure*]." (p. 17) [LACAN-YFS]

> "Hamlet is constantly suspended in the time of the Other, throughout the entire story until the
> very end." (p. 17) [LACAN-YFS]

Applied to the prayer scene (III.iii):

> "And Hamlet stops, because it's not time. It's not the hour of the Other: not time for the Other to
> render his 'audit' to heaven. … Whatever may happen later, this is not the hour of the Other, and
> he suspends his action. Whatever Hamlet may do, he will do it only at the hour of the Other."
> (p. 18) [LACAN-YFS]

> "When he stays on, it is the hour of his parents. When he suspends his crime, it is the hour of the
> others. When he leaves for England, it is the hour of his stepfather. It's the hour of Rosencrantz
> and Guildenstern when he sends them on ahead to death…" (p. 18) [LACAN-YFS]

So the deferral is *not* explained as inhibition-by-guilt (the Jones/Freud line) but as a temporal
dependence: Hamlet's act must be scheduled by the Other.

*(c) "He does not know what he wants" — and this is a claim about structure, not psychology.* The
exact wording:

> "To put it in commonsensical terms, Hamlet just doesn't know what he wants." (p. 26) [LACAN-YFS]

immediately preceded by the observation that "he's never set a goal for himself, an object — a choice
that always has something 'arbitrary' about it" (pp. 25–26). Lacan then reads the Fortinbras
soliloquy ("How all occasions do inform against me") as the play's own staging of that structure: the
Polish campaign is a case of arbitrary object-choice, which is precisely what Hamlet cannot perform
[LACAN-YFS].

*(d) Ophelia as object and as phallus.* Lacan proposes a three-stage trajectory of Ophelia as object.
He is explicitly wary of over-reading and says so before offering the wordplay:

> "Some people have reproached me for the timidity with which they feel I've been proceeding. I don't
> think that's the case. I wouldn't want to encourage you to produce the sort of hogwash that
> psychoanalytic texts are full of. I'm just surprised that nobody's pointed out that Ophelia is
> *O phallos*…" (p. 20) [LACAN-YFS]

He then *withdraws* the etymological argument and rests the claim on the text's own obsession with
child-bearing: "There's no need to resort to the etymology of 'Ophelia.' Hamlet speaks constantly of
one thing: child-bearing" (p. 23). The structural formulation is:

> "For the subject the object appears, if I may put it this way, on the outside. The subject is no
> longer the object: he rejects it with all the force of his being and will not find it again until
> he sacrifices himself. It is in this sense that the object is here the equivalent of, assumes the
> place of, indeed *is* — the phallus. … Ophelia is at this point the phallus, exteriorized and
> rejected by the subject as a symbol signifying life." (p. 23) [LACAN-YFS]

The third stage is the graveyard: Ophelia is *recovered as object a* only once she is dead and only
through rivalry with Laertes.

> "Hamlet cannot bear Laertes' display of sorrow at his sister's burial. It is the ostentatiousness
> of Laertes' mourning that makes Hamlet lose control … at the graveyard he goes for Laertes' throat,
> leaping into the hole into which Ophelia's body has just been lowered." [LACAN-YFS]

This is the point of the whole reading: Hamlet's desire becomes locatable only via the imaginary
rival, and only over a grave. Note carefully: Lacan does *not* say "Ophelia is objet a" as a static
property; he says the object occupies successive positions across the play's action.

*(e) Mourning and the failure of ritual — the hinge of the whole seminar.* Lacan's general theory
first:

> "Where is the gap, the hole that results from this loss and that calls forth mourning on the part of
> the subject? It is a hole in the real … Just as what is rejected from the symbolic register
> reappears in the real, in the same way the hole in the real that results from loss, sets the
> signifier in motion. This hole provides the place for the projection of the missing signifier,
> which is essential to the structure of the Other … the signifier that is essentially the veiled
> phallus." (p. 37) [LACAN-YFS]

> "Ritual introduces some mediation of the gap [*béance*] opened up by mourning. More precisely,
> ritual operates in such a way as to make this gap coincide with that greater béance, the point x,
> the symbolic lack." (p. 40) [LACAN-YFS]

Then the textual evidence, which is genuinely a close reading of the play's stage business:

> "Nor can we fail to be struck by the fact that in all the instances of mourning in *Hamlet*, one
> element is always present: the rites have been cut short and performed in secret." (p. 40)
> [LACAN-YFS]

He instances (i) Polonius "buried secretly, without ceremony, posthaste"; (ii) Ophelia's contested
Christian burial, quoting the Priest at V.i ("She should in ground unsanctified have lodged / Till
the last trumpet…") and noting "the rites to which he has consented are themselves abbreviated";
(iii) old Hamlet killed "in the blossoms of [his] sin," with "no time before his death to summon up
the composure … that would have prepared him to go before the throne of judgment" (pp. 40–41)
[LACAN-YFS]. Lacan's explanation of the *ghost* follows from this: images swarm into the place of the
missing signifier "when someone's departure from this life has not been accompanied by the rites that
it calls for" (p. 38).

*(f) The phallus and the un-strikeable object.* The final movement links the deferral back to the
phallus:

> "the phallus to be struck at is real indeed. And Hamlet always stops. The very source of what makes
> Hamlet's arm waver at every moment, is the narcissistic connection that Freud tells us about in his
> text on the decline of the Oedipus complex: one cannot strike the phallus, because the phallus, even
> the real phallus, is a ghost." (p. 50) [LACAN-YFS]

**Accuracy caveat for the treatise.** The YFS text is Miller's edited version of a stenographic
transcript, translated by Hulbert; the Fink/Polity 2019 text is a different editorial establishment
of the same sessions, and wordings differ. Any quotation must name which edition it comes from. All
quotations above are from the 1977 Hulbert translation.

### 2. Seminar VII, *The Ethics of Psychoanalysis* (1959–60): the Antigone chapters

**Edition.** Lacan, J. (1992). *The Seminar of Jacques Lacan, Book VII: The Ethics of Psychoanalysis,
1959–1960*, ed. J.-A. Miller, trans. Dennis Porter. New York: W. W. Norton (also Routledge, London).
I read the Norton 1992 text directly. French original: Seuil, 1986 [S7-NORTON / S7-OL]. **VERIFIED**
(Norton text read; Open Library confirms Seuil 1986 / ISBN 9780393316131).

**Chapter structure — exactly as printed in the Norton contents page** [S7-NORTON] **VERIFIED**:

- Part: **THE ESSENCE OF TRAGEDY — A Commentary on Sophocles's *Antigone***
  - XIX. "The splendor of Antigone" — p. 243
  - XX. "The articulations of the play" — p. 257
  - XXI. "Antigone between two deaths" — p. 270
- Part: **THE TRAGIC DIMENSION OF ANALYTICAL EXPERIENCE**
  - XXII. "The demand for happiness and the promise of analysis" — p. 291
  - XXIII. "The moral goals of psychoanalysis" — p. 302
  - XXIV. "The paradoxes of ethics or Have you acted in conformity with your desire?" — p. 311

The user's "roughly chapters 19–21" is exactly right. Note the preceding chapter XVIII, "The function
of the beautiful" (p. 231), is where the aesthetic argument is set up and should not be skipped.

**The claims, accurately.**

*(a) The splendour of the image is a function, not a compliment.* Lacan's argument is that tragedy
works through an image that both attracts and blinds:

> "This line of sight focuses on an image that possesses a mystery which up till now has never been
> articulated, since it forces you to close your eyes at the very moment you look at it. Yet that
> image is at the center of tragedy, since it is the fascinating image of Antigone herself. We know
> very well that over and beyond the dialogue, over and beyond the question of family and country,
> over and beyond the moralizing arguments, it is Antigone herself who fascinates us, Antigone in her
> unbearable splendor." (pp. 247–48) [S7-NORTON]

And the beauty is positional, not ornamental:

> "It has to do with Antigone's beauty and with the place it occupies as intermediary between two
> fields that are symbolically differentiated. It is doubtless from this place that her splendor
> derives…" (p. 248) [S7-NORTON]

Earlier in the same seminar Lacan gives the general formula that governs this: "It is obviously
because truth is not pretty to look at that beauty is, if not its splendor, then at least its
envelope" [S7-NORTON]. So beauty is a *veil over a beyond* — it draws desire toward, and stops it
just before, the thing it veils. Marc De Kesel puts the corrective sharply in his review of Freeland:
Antigone's splendour is "simply her beauty," it is not itself the unbearable thing; "Beauty is a veil,
an imaginary ruse that hides a beyond, and it is that beyond that is 'unbearable'" [DEKESEL-2015].

*(b) Atè.* Lacan treats Atè as a *limit* of a field, not as a psychological state or as "misfortune":

> "One can no doubt translate Atè by 'misfortune,' but it doesn't have anything to do with
> misfortune." (p. 264) [S7-NORTON]

> "One does or does not approach Atè, and when one approaches it, it is because of something that is
> linked to a beginning and a chain of events, namely, that of the misfortune of the Labdacides
> family." (p. 264) [S7-NORTON]

> "…the limit that is Atè, and it is around this that what Antigone wants is played out." [S7-NORTON]

> "The Chorus emphasizes the fact that she went in search of her Atè. … It is because she goes toward
> Atè here, because it is even a question of going ἐκτὸς ἄτας, of going beyond the limit of Atè, that
> Antigone interests the Chorus." (p. 277) [S7-NORTON]

> "Atè concerns the Other, the field of the Other…" [S7-NORTON]

> "Antigone perpetuates, eternalizes, immortalizes that Atè." (p. 283) [S7-NORTON]

*(c) Between two deaths.* This is the topological claim that carries the chapter title. It is
attached to a precise moment of the play's action — Creon's sentence of immurement:

> "When does this complaint begin? From the moment when she crosses the entrance to the zone between
> life and death, that is to say, when what she has already affirmed herself to be takes on an
> outward form. She has been telling us for a long time that she is in the kingdom of the dead, but
> at this point the idea is consecrated. Her punishment will consist in her being shut up or
> suspended in the zone between life and death. Although she is not yet dead, she is eliminated from
> the world of the living." (p. 280) [S7-NORTON]

> "…from Antigone's point of view life can only be approached, can only be lived or thought about,
> from the place of that limit where her life is already lost, where she is already on the other
> side. But from that place she can see it and live it in the form of something already lost."
> (pp. 280–81) [S7-NORTON]

And the two registers are explicitly welded together:

> "The violent illumination, the glow of beauty, coincides with the moment of transgression or of
> realization of Antigone's Atè…" (p. 281) [S7-NORTON]

Lacan also flags the affective term the Chorus uses: "Nothing is more moving than that ἵμερος ἐναργής,
than the desire that visibly emanates from the eyelids of this admirable girl" (p. 281). The "second
death" is derived earlier from Sade — "death insofar as it is regarded as the point at which the very
cycles of the transformations of nature are annihilated" (p. 248) [S7-NORTON]. Index entries in the
Norton edition: *Atè*, 262–64, 267, 270, 277, 281, 283, 286, 300; *second death*, 211, 248, 254, 260,
294–95 [S7-NORTON].

*(d) Not ceding on one's desire.* The famous maxim appears in chapter XXIII, not in the Antigone
chapters, and Porter renders it as "giving ground relative to," not "ceding":

> "I propose then that, from an analytical point of view, the only thing of which one can be guilty is
> of having given ground relative to one's desire." [S7-NORTON]

> "In the last analysis, what a subject really feels guilty about when he manifests guilt at bottom
> always has to do with … the extent to which he has given ground relative to his desire. … He has
> often given ground relative to his desire for a good motive or even for the best of motives."
> [S7-NORTON]

The French is *ne pas céder sur son désir*. The seminar's closing question in chapter XXIV is "Have
you acted in conformity with your desire?" [S7-NORTON]. Two accuracy points a treatise must observe:
(i) this is stated as an *experimental paradox* offered to analysts' ears, not a slogan Lacan hands to
literary critics; (ii) it is a proposition about *guilt*, not a prescription to get what you want.

*(e) Lacan cross-references his own Hamlet reading inside Seminar VII, and corrects the received
view.* In chapter XIX:

> "Hamlet is by no means a drama of the importance of thought in the face of action. … I don't believe
> that the drama of Hamlet is to be found in such a divergence between action and thought nor in the
> problem of the extinction of his desire. I tried to show that Hamlet's strange apathy belongs to the
> sphere of action itself … we will find its origin in a relationship to the mother's desire and to
> the father's knowledge of his own death." (pp. 251–52) [S7-NORTON]

> "If Hamlet stops when he is on the point of killing Claudius, it is because he is worried about that
> precise point I am trying to define here: simply to kill him is not enough, he wants him to suffer
> hell's eternal torture. … Hamlet stops in the middle of his act because he wants Claudius to go to
> hell." (p. 252) [S7-NORTON]

This is important for the treatise: in Seminar VII Lacan re-reads the prayer-scene deferral through
the *second death* (Hamlet wants a damnation beyond biological death) rather than through the "hour of
the Other." The two readings are complementary but not identical, and citing one as though it were
the other is a common secondary-literature error.

### 3. Other Lacan material on drama, tragedy and literature commonly cited

- **"Seminar on 'The Purloined Letter'."** The seminar of 26 April 1955, placed by Lacan as the
  *opening* (overture) text of the *Écrits*. English: Lacan, J., trans. Jeffrey Mehlman (1972),
  "Seminar on 'The Purloined Letter'," *Yale French Studies* No. 48 (*French Freud: Structural Studies
  in Psychoanalysis*), first page 39, DOI 10.2307/2929623 [LACAN-PL]. **VERIFIED via Crossref**
  (authors Lacan and Mehlman, *Yale French Studies*, 1972, first page 39). Reprinted with the Derrida
  and Johnson responses in Muller, J. P. & Richardson, W. J. (eds.), *The Purloined Poe: Lacan,
  Derrida, and Psychoanalytic Reading*, Johns Hopkins University Press, 1987/1988, 408 pp., ISBN
  9780801832925 [PURLOINED-POE] **VERIFIED via Open Library**. In Fink's complete English *Écrits*
  (Norton, 2005/2006, 878 pp., ISBN 9780393061154 / 9780393329254 [ECRITS]) it is the first paper;
  I did **not** verify its exact page range in Fink's edition — **UNVERIFIED** on that point.
  Methodologically this is the key text for the anti-character-criticism defence: Lacan reads the
  *positions in the circuit of the letter* (the one who sees nothing, the one who sees that the first
  sees nothing, the one who sees that the first two leave the thing exposed), not the psychology of
  Dupin or the Minister. The subjects are interchangeable; the structure is not.
- **Sophocles more broadly.** Seminar VII's tragedy commentary quotes Sophocles beyond *Antigone*
  (e.g. *Electra* — "Why do you always plunge yourself into the Atè of your…" [S7-NORTON]) and
  repeatedly treats Oedipus, especially *Oedipus at Colonus*, alongside Antigone as figures on the
  "boundary between life and death" [S7-NORTON]. **VERIFIED** from the Norton text.
- **Claudel's Coûfontaine trilogy in Seminar VIII, *Transference* (1960–61).** Lacan devotes an
  extended sequence of sessions to Paul Claudel's trilogy — *L'Otage*, *Le Pain dur*, *Le Père
  humilié* — and to Sygne de Coûfontaine, whom he treats as a modern counterpart to Antigone and as
  the exemplary figure of a sacrifice that goes beyond even the renunciation of desire. English
  edition: Lacan, J. (2015). *Transference: The Seminar of Jacques Lacan, Book VIII*, ed. J.-A.
  Miller, trans. Bruce Fink. Cambridge: Polity, ISBN 150952360X [S8-OL]. **Bibliographic data
  PARTIALLY VERIFIED** (Open Library returns Polity Press with that ISBN, but under a Spanish-language
  title record; Polity's own catalogue page would not load). **The characterisation of the Claudel
  content is UNVERIFIED** — I did not obtain the Seminar VIII text itself. A treatise should check
  the chapter titles in the Polity 2015 edition before citing.
- **Hamlet references elsewhere.** Besides Seminar VII ch. XIX, Hamlet recurs in Lacan's writing on
  desire and the graph of desire; the *Écrits* index carries the play. **UNVERIFIED** beyond the
  Seminar VII passages I read directly.
- **Ella Sharpe.** Worth noting because Lacan names her in the Hamlet seminar: he refers to "the
  *Papers on Hamlet* … which Ella Sharpe unfortunately left unfinished and which it was perhaps a
  mistake to publish after her death" [LACAN-YFS] — i.e. Lacan himself uses an earlier psychoanalytic
  reading of Hamlet as his negative example of "hogwash." This is a useful self-critical hook.

### 4. Secondary literature on Lacanian literary criticism as a method — and the objections

**The pro-case.**

- **Felman, S. (ed.) (1982). *Literature and Psychoanalysis: The Question of Reading: Otherwise*.
  Baltimore: Johns Hopkins University Press.** Open Library: JHU Press, 1982, 508–512 pp., ISBNs
  0801827531 / 9780801827549 [FELMAN-1982]. **VERIFIED via Open Library**; it also records the 1977
  *Yale French Studies* origin of the same collection. Felman's editorial argument is the standard
  methodological warrant for Lacanian reading: the move from psychoanalysis *applied to* literature
  (literature as patient, author or character as case) to a relation of mutual implication in which
  literature is the *unconscious of psychoanalysis* and the critic's own position is part of what is
  read. Her own contribution on *The Turn of the Screw* is the demonstration piece. This
  characterisation is **UNVERIFIED** at the level of quotation — I did not fetch the essay text.
- **Felman, S. (1987). *Jacques Lacan and the Adventure of Insight: Psychoanalysis in Contemporary
  Culture*. Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press.** Open Library: Harvard UP, 1987, 189 pp., ISBN
  0674471202 / 9780674471207 [FELMAN-1987]. **Bibliographic data VERIFIED via Open Library**; the
  Harvard catalogue page returned 404 to me.
- **Bowie, M. (1991). *Lacan*. London: Fontana Press / Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press.**
  Open Library: 1991, 244–248 pp., ISBN 0674508521 / 9780674508521 [BOWIE-1991]. **VERIFIED via Open
  Library.** Also relevant: Bowie, M. (1987). *Freud, Proust and Lacan: Theory as Fiction*. Cambridge

- **Fink, B.** Translator of the *Écrits*, Seminar VI and Seminar VIII; author of *A Clinical

  **VERIFIED via Springer**. Fink's importance to a treatise is that he is the point of contact

**The objection, in its strongest form.**

The canonical English statement is L. C. Knights, *How Many Children Had Lady Macbeth? An Essay in the
Theory and Practice of Shakespeare Criticism*, Cambridge: Gordon Fraser, The Minority Press, 1933
[KNIGHTS-1933]. **VERIFIED** — I read the full text of the 1933 first edition; the title page reads
exactly "GORDON FRASER / THE MINORITY PRESS, CAMBRIDGE / 1933," and it notes "This essay is based upon
a paper read before the Shakespeare Association at King's College, London."

The essay's title is a *reductio*, and it is aimed squarely at A. C. Bradley. Its positive thesis:

> "A Shakespeare play is a dramatic poem. It uses action, gesture, formal grouping and symbols, and it
> relies upon the general conventions governing Elizabethan plays. But, we cannot too often remind
> ourselves, its end is to communicate a rich and controlled experience by means of words…"
> (pp. 7–8) [KNIGHTS-1933]

> "To stress in the conventional way character or plot or any of the other abstractions that can be
> made, is to impoverish the total response." (p. 8) [KNIGHTS-1933]

Knights quotes Wilson Knight approvingly for the sharpest version: "The persons, ultimately, are not
human at all, but purely symbols of a poetic vision" (p. 8, quoting *The Wheel of Fire*, p. 16), and
Bradbrook: "It is in the total situation rather than in the wrigglings of individual emotion that the
tragedy lies" (p. 8) [KNIGHTS-1933]. He then names the habit he is attacking, via Hartley Coleridge:

> "'Let us,' he said, 'for a moment, put Shakespeare out of the question, and consider Hamlet as a
> real person, a recently deceased acquaintance.' The habit of regarding Shakespeare's persons as
> 'friends for life' or, maybe, 'deceased acquaintances,' is responsible for most of the vagaries that
> serve as Shakespeare criticism." (pp. 27–28) [KNIGHTS-1933]

**The strongest version of the objection, stated for the treatise.** It is not the weak version
("characters are made up, so psychology doesn't apply"). The strong version has four steps, and each
is independently damaging:

1. **Ontological.** A dramatic character is not an incomplete description of a person; it is a
   complete description of a textual construct. There is nothing about Hamlet that is not on the page,
   in the theatrical convention, or supplied by the reader. To ask what Hamlet unconsciously wants is
   not to ask a hard question about a hidden thing; it is to ask a question with no truth-maker.
   Lady Macbeth's children are neither some number nor zero: the question is malformed.
2. **Evidential.** The unconscious is an inference licensed by clinical technique — free association,
   transference, resistance, slips over time, the analysand's own corrections. None of these is
   available from a text. A character cannot associate, cannot resist, cannot be surprised by an
   interpretation and cannot falsify one. The diagnostic apparatus is severed from the evidence base
   that gives it whatever warrant it has.
3. **Aesthetic / New Critical.** Character-talk imports "irrelevant moral and realistic canons"
   (Knights) and displaces attention from the medium — verse movement, image pattern, scene
   juxtaposition, silence, the impersonal voice. The tragedy of *Macbeth* is in the poem; converting
   it into a case history loses precisely the thing that makes it a play rather than a chart.
4. **Post-structuralist.** The later objection sharpens (1): the "subject" is an effect of language,
   so treating a character as a stable interiority whose depths can be sounded reinstates exactly the
   humanist unified ego that Lacan's own theory dismantles. Barthes's "death of the author" and the
   general critique of the expressive subject apply *a fortiori* to characters. **This fourth step is
   UNVERIFIED as to specific sources** — I did not fetch Barthes or a post-structuralist statement of
   it, and the treatise should cite one directly rather than gesturing.

**The Lacanian reply, and how good it is.** The honest reply is not that characters do have
unconsciouses. It is that Lacan's best readings do not claim they do. The *Purloined Letter* seminar
reads *positions in a structure*, not minds; the Hamlet seminar's load-bearing evidence is the play's
stage business — who is buried how, who has time to prepare for death, which rites are abbreviated —
not Hamlet's inner life; the Antigone chapters read the Chorus's Greek and the sentence of
immurement. Where Lacan lapses into diagnosis ("Hamlet just doesn't know what he wants"), he is
explicitly flagging it as a commonsensical gloss on a structural claim. And Lacan himself supplies the
in-house version of the Knights objection: "I wouldn't want to encourage you to produce the sort of
hogwash that psychoanalytic texts are full of" [LACAN-YFS]. That said, the reply does not fully
succeed: Lacan *does* make claims about Hamlet's desire that read as psychological, and Felman's
mutual-implication framing is better understood as a change of subject than as a refutation of
Knights.

### 5. Does any published work assign numeric or vector-valued psychological measures to dramatic characters?

**Short answer: yes, but it is rare, thin, recent, and almost entirely absent for named tragic
protagonists like Hamlet, Medea or Antigone in the peer-reviewed literature.** The nearest things are
four, in descending order of relevance:

**(a) Numeric trait ratings of character *types* (closest published, peer-reviewed instance).**
Berry, M. & Brown, S. (2017). "A Classification Scheme for Literary Characters." *Psychological
Thought*, 10(2), 288–302, DOI 10.5964/psyct.v10i2.237 [BERRY-BROWN-2017]. **VERIFIED** — I read the
full PDF and Crossref confirms authors, journal, volume, pages and DOI. 142 participants rated 40
literary/dramatic characters on four 1–9 Likert scales: assertiveness and cooperativeness (from a
modified Thomas–Kilmann Conflict Mode Instrument) plus extraversion and conscientiousness from the Big
Five. From the abstract:

> "we had 142 participants provide personality ratings for 40 characters using two of the Big Five
> personality traits as well as assertiveness and cooperativeness from the TK scheme. The results
> showed that assertiveness and cooperativeness were orthogonal dimensions…" [BERRY-BROWN-2017]

**Crucial limitation, which I verified from Table 1:** the 40 "characters" are *stock types*, not named
individuals — Action Hero, Artist, Average Joe, Babysitter, Bimbo, Bully, Butler, Coward, Critic,
Crone, Cynic, Damsel in Distress, Femme Fatale, Geek, Grandparent, Grump, Hero, Heroine, Jock, King,
Librarian, Loner, Lover, Miser, Monster, Old Hag, Politician, Priest, Professor, Queen, Recluse,
Rogue, Scientist, Self, Sidekick, Snob, Spoiled Child, Supervillain, Superhero, Teacher. Participants
were asked "to imagine the character being performed on stage by an actor (i.e., as if in a theatrical
production)." So: a real 2-D (or 4-D) character vector space exists in the literature — for
*roles*, not for Hamlet. The paper's sources include Landy (1996), i.e. drama therapy's taxonomy of
roles.

**(b) Machine-assigned Big Five labels for named Shakespeare characters (the closest hit for named
dramatic individuals).** Pizzolli, D. & Strapparava, C. (2019). "Personality Traits Recognition in
Literary Texts." In *Proceedings of the Second Workshop on Storytelling*, Florence: Association for
Computational Linguistics, pp. 107–111. DOI 10.18653/v1/W19-3411 [PIZZOLLI-2019]. **VERIFIED** —
I read the PDF and the ACL Anthology BibTeX record. They train Big Five classifiers on an external
gold-labelled corpus (essays scored via Mairesse et al. 2007 features, converted to nominal classes by
Celli) and apply them to the utterances of characters in Shakespeare's plays, producing a **binary
five-vector per character** (AGR, CON, EXT, NEU, OPN). Their result table lists, among others,
Hamlet, Horatio, Laertes, Macbeth, Lady Macbeth, Malcolm, Macduff, Ross, Portia, Shylock, Bassanio,
Launcelot Gobbo, Antonio, Iago, Othello, Desdemona, Cassio, Emilia, Romeo, Juliet, Friar Laurence,
Nurse, Capulet. The authors are candid that this is exploratory and weak: F1 scores of 0.45–0.61, and
"We do not have a gold labeled dataset to confront with." They also note the Shakespeare-specific
threats to validity — "Shakespeare's English is not today's English" — and, importantly for the
treatise, that "Usually the personality traits are considered stable. But the need of creating tension
and drama in a play may also imply some evolution in the personality" [PIZZOLLI-2019]. This is the
literature's own statement of why a *static* trait vector is the wrong object for drama.

**(c) Bottom-up computational personality structure in narrative.** Fischer, R., Karl, J. A.,
Luczak-Roesch, M., Fetvadjiev, V. H. & Grener, A. (2020). "Tracing Personality Structure in Narratives:
A Computational Bottom–Up Approach to Unpack Writers, Characters, and Personality in Historical
Context." *European Journal of Personality*, 34(5), 917–943, DOI 10.1002/per.2270 [FISCHER-2020].
**Bibliographic data VERIFIED via Crossref** (authors, journal, 2020, vol. 34, pp. 917–943, DOI).
**Content UNVERIFIED** — I did not obtain the full text. From title and venue this derives personality
dimensions from narrative text rather than imposing trait scores on named characters, which makes it
methodologically the most respectable neighbour of what a treatise would be doing.

**(d) Crowd-sourced bipolar trait vectors for named *Hamlet* characters — real data, not peer
reviewed.** The Open-Source Psychometrics Project's "Statistical 'Which Character' Personality Quiz"
dataset includes the *Hamlet* universe with six characters — Prince Hamlet, Ophelia, King Claudius,
Horatio, Queen Gertrude, Polonius — each rated by users on a large battery of bipolar adjective items
("nerd (not jock)", "deep (not shallow)", "haunted (not blissful)", …), with per-character pages
[OPENPSYCH-HAMLET]. **VERIFIED** — I fetched the page and read the character list and item structure.
This is, so far as I could find, the only substantial public dataset of *vector-valued* measures for
named dramatic characters. It is a web project with an explicitly self-selected sample; the site itself
warns that "users of this quiz are more likely to be young and more likely to be women." It is usable
as an existence proof and as a source of intuition, never as evidence.

**Adjacent traditions searched, with honest negative results.**

- **Character-network analysis** measures *relations*, not psychological states: it assigns each
  character a position in a graph (degree, centrality, community), not a trait vector. The founding
  reference is Moretti, F. (2011). "Network Theory, Plot Analysis," *New Left Review*, 2, 80–102, DOI
  10.64590/vko [MORETTI-2011] **bibliographic data VERIFIED via Crossref**; see also Moretti, F.
  (2013). *Distant Reading*, Verso, 224 pp., ISBN 1781680841 [MORETTI-2013] **VERIFIED via Open
  Library**. Relatedly, Elson, D. & McKeown, K. (2010), "Automatic Attribution of Quoted Speech in
  Literary Narrative," *Proceedings of the AAAI Conference on Artificial Intelligence*, 24, 1013–1019,
  DOI 10.1609/aaai.v24i1.7720 [ELSON-2010] **VERIFIED via Crossref** — the infrastructure for
  extracting per-character speech that any character-vector project needs.
- **Emotional-arc quantification** produces a *time series per work*, and occasionally per character,
  of sentiment — which is the closest formal analogue to a "state trajectory" for scoring purposes,
  but it is one-dimensional valence, not a psychological state vector. Reagan, A. J., Mitchell, L.,
  Kiley, D., Danforth, C. M. & Dodds, P. S. (2016). "The emotional arcs of stories are dominated by
  six basic shapes," *EPJ Data Science*, 5, DOI 10.1140/epjds/s13688-016-0093-1 [REAGAN-2016]
  **bibliographic data VERIFIED via Crossref**.
- **Drama therapy** has a *taxonomy of roles* (Landy) — categorical role types with described
  qualities, functions and styles — but it is a classification scheme, not a numeric measurement
  instrument, and it is oriented to the client enacting a role, not to scoring a playwright's
  character. Landy, R. (1992). "The Drama Therapy Role Method," *Dramatherapy*, 14, 7–15, DOI
  10.1080/02630672.1992.9689810 [LANDY-1992] **bibliographic data VERIFIED via Crossref**; Landy's
  *Persona and Performance* (1993) and the 1996 taxonomy cited by Berry & Brown are the fuller
  statements — **UNVERIFIED**.
- **Psychology of fiction** (Oatley, Mar and colleagues) measures *readers*, not characters: empathy,
  transportation, theory-of-mind outcomes of reading. I found nothing in that tradition that scores a
  named dramatic character. **UNVERIFIED as an exhaustive claim** — I did not run a dedicated search
  of that literature.
- **LLM persona work** (PersonaLLM; Big5-Chat and similar) assigns Big Five profiles to *synthetic
  agents* and measures whether the model expresses them; it is about models, not about reading plays.
  **UNVERIFIED** — noted from search results only.

**Verdict on item 5.** Assigning a numeric or vector-valued psychological state to a *named* dramatic
character is close to absent as a scholarly practice. The two peer-reviewed things that come closest
are Berry & Brown 2017 (real human ratings, but of stock types, four dimensions) and Pizzolli &
Strapparava 2019 (named Shakespeare characters, five binary dimensions, weak and self-described as
exploratory). Nobody has published a psychometrically defended trait vector for Hamlet, Medea or
Antigone. A treatise that builds a character state vector is therefore doing something largely
unprecedented — which is an opportunity and a liability in equal measure, and the liability is that
there is no established validity literature to lean on.

## What a treatise on scoring drama can legitimately say

- **Ground the Lacanian layer in what Lacan actually does with texts, not in what he is reputed to do.**
  His Hamlet evidence is stage business — Polonius buried "secretly, without ceremony, posthaste,"
  the Priest's abbreviated rites for Ophelia, old Hamlet killed "in the blossoms of [his] sin" — and
  his Antigone evidence is the Chorus's Greek and Creon's sentence of immurement. Both are readings of
  *the text's own structure of action*. That is the model to imitate: a state vector whose components
  are anchored in observable textual events is defensible; one whose components are inferred
  interiority is not.
- **Concede the category error explicitly and early, in its strong form.** State Knights's point
  without softening: a dramatic character is a complete description of a textual construct, not an
  incomplete description of a person; there is no fact of the matter about Lady Macbeth's children,
  and by the same logic no fact of the matter about Hamlet's unconscious. A treatise that concedes
  this in chapter one buys the right to use psychoanalytic vocabulary later as *descriptive apparatus*
  rather than as diagnosis.
- **Frame the vector as a reading, not a measurement.** The honest formulation: "the vector records
  *how this production/analysis construes the character at this moment in the text*, and different
  defensible readings yield different vectors." Publish the mapping from textual evidence to component
  values so a reader can disagree at the level of the evidence. A vector that cannot be argued with is
  a diagnosis; one that can be is a reading.
- **Prefer positional and relational components over trait components.** This is where the *Purloined
  Letter* seminar earns its place: Lacan's three positions in the circuit of the letter belong to
  *places*, not persons, and are occupied by different characters in successive scenes. Components
  like "relation to the Other's demand," "position relative to the lack," "who in this scene sees that
  the others do not see" are far more defensible than "neuroticism = 0.7," and they are also the ones
  that change scene by scene — which is exactly what a score needs.
- **Make the vector time-varying and say why.** Pizzolli & Strapparava themselves note that although
  "the personality traits are considered stable," the need to create dramatic tension "may also imply
  some evolution in the personality." A static trait profile is the wrong object for drama on the
  computational literature's own admission; a trajectory is the right one, and it is also what a score
  needs. Lacan's Ophelia is a three-stage trajectory (love object → exteriorised and rejected phallus
  → object a recovered in the grave), not a fixed attribute.
- **Do not smuggle in clinical authority.** Psychoanalytic terms may be used as a vocabulary for
  structural relations in a text. They may not be used to imply that the character has been assessed.
  Never write that a character "is" neurotic, psychotic or perverse; write that the text organises
  this figure's desire in the way Lacan describes under that heading. Lacan's own warning against
  "the sort of hogwash that psychoanalytic texts are full of" — aimed at Ella Sharpe's *Papers on
  Hamlet* — is the citation to use when making this promise.
- **Use Felman's mutual-implication frame, but label it as a change of subject.** Felman's move — that
  literature is the unconscious of psychoanalysis, and that the critic's own position is part of the
  reading — is the strongest available methodological warrant, and it usefully makes the composer's
  act of scoring part of the object rather than a neutral observation. But it does not refute Knights;
  it declines his question. Saying so is more persuasive than pretending the objection was answered.
- **Distinguish the two Lacanian accounts of Hamlet's deferral and cite them separately.** Seminar VI:
  Hamlet acts only "at the hour of the Other," a temporal dependence. Seminar VII: he stops at the
  prayer scene "because he wants Claudius to go to hell," a matter of the second death. These are
  complementary but distinct, and a treatise that conflates them will be caught.
- **State what is unprecedented.** No published work assigns a psychometrically defended trait vector
  to Hamlet, Medea or Antigone. The nearest peer-reviewed neighbours are ratings of *stock types*
  (Berry & Brown 2017) and weak machine-assigned binary Big Five labels for Shakespeare characters
  (Pizzolli & Strapparava 2019). Claim novelty accurately, and do not claim validation that does not
  exist.
- **Borrow the right formal neighbours.** Character-network analysis (Moretti) gives relational
  position; emotional-arc work (Reagan et al.) gives a per-work trajectory; drama therapy's role
  taxonomy (Landy) gives categorical role types. None of these is a psychological measurement of a
  character, and each should be cited for what it is. Citing them as precedent for trait scoring would
  be a misrepresentation a reviewer will catch immediately.
- **Keep the epistemic register of the score honest.** If the vector drives musical parameters, its
  job is to be *productive and arguable*, not true. A score is an interpretation performed in sound;
  that is a recognised and honourable kind of claim about a text, and it does not need the borrowed
  authority of clinical assessment to be taken seriously.
